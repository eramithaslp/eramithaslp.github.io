    Resistors, variable resistors, digital potentiometers and capacitors

    B.Sc. B2.42.5 hoursUnit 1

    Every instrument in a speech and hearing clinic, from a pocket hearing aid to a clinical audiometer, is built from a small family of basic parts. This topic starts from the very beginning (charge, voltage, current and resistance), then explains fixed resistors, variable resistors such as the volume control, the digital potentiometers that let a microcontroller set a level, and finally capacitors, which store charge and let AC signals pass while blocking DC.

    What it is

    A resistor is a component that opposes the flow of electric current by a fixed, known amount. A variable resistor (potentiometer, rheostat or trimmer) lets that amount be changed by turning a knob or a screw. A digital potentiometer is an integrated circuit that does the same job with electronic switches, controlled by a microcontroller. A capacitor is a pair of conducting plates separated by an insulator; it stores electric charge and its behaviour depends on frequency.

    Why it is required

    Signals in audiology equipment must be set to exact levels: the hearing level dial of an audiometer, the gain of a hearing aid and the recording level of a speech analysis system all rely on controlled division of a voltage. Capacitors are needed to pass speech signals from one stage to the next without passing DC, to smooth power supplies, and they are the very heart of the condenser microphone used in sound level meters.

    How it works

    A resistor converts part of the electrical energy into heat and sets the current according to Ohm's law. Two resistors in series divide a voltage in a fixed ratio; a potentiometer makes that ratio adjustable. A digital potentiometer selects one tap on a chain of tiny resistors. A capacitor charges when a voltage is applied, taking a time set by the resistance and capacitance, and its opposition to AC (reactance) falls as frequency rises.

    Starting from the basics: charge, voltage, current and resistance

    All matter contains electric charge. Electrons carry a tiny negative charge and protons an equal positive charge. Charge is measured in coulombs (C); one electron carries about 1.6 × 10-19 C, so one coulomb is the charge of roughly 6.24 × 1018 electrons. In a metal wire some electrons (called free electrons) are loosely held and can drift from atom to atom.

    A helpful picture is water in a pipe. The amount of water flowing past a point each second is like the current. The pressure difference that pushes the water is like the voltage. A narrow section of pipe that restricts the flow is like a resistance.

    By convention, conventional current is drawn flowing from the positive terminal of a source, through the circuit, to the negative terminal. The electrons actually drift the other way. Circuit diagrams and all formulas in these notes use conventional current. Direct current (DC), as from a battery, flows in one direction all the time. Alternating current (AC), as from the mains or from a microphone, reverses direction many times per second; the number of complete cycles per second is the frequency in hertz (Hz).

    Ohm's law

    For most metals and for resistors at a steady temperature, the current is proportional to the voltage. This is Ohm's law:

    V = I × R I = V / R R = V / I where V is the voltage across the resistor in volts, I the current through it in amperes and R its resistance in ohms.

    Electrical energy turned into heat in a resistor each second is the power:

    P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R where P is in watts. The resistor must be rated for at least this power, or it overheats.

    Resistance of a wire or a resistive track depends on its material and shape: R = ρ L / A, where ρ (rho) is the resistivity of the material, L the length and A the cross-sectional area. A longer, thinner track has more resistance. This simple fact is how every potentiometer works: moving the wiper changes the length of track between two terminals.

    Worked example: a 9 V battery is connected across a 1.5 kΩ resistor. Current I = 9 / 1500 = 0.006 A = 6 mA. Power P = 9 × 0.006 = 0.054 W = 54 mW, so an ordinary 0.25 W resistor is more than adequate.

    Series and parallel

    Resistors joined end to end are in series: the same current flows through all of them and the total resistance is the sum, RT = R1 + R2 + ... . Resistors joined across the same two points are in parallel: they share the same voltage and the total resistance is smaller than the smallest one, 1/RT = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ... . For two in parallel this becomes RT = R1R2 / (R1 + R2). Two 10 kΩ resistors give 20 kΩ in series and 5 kΩ in parallel.

    Fixed resistors

    A fixed resistor has one value that cannot be adjusted. Common constructions are:

    • Carbon film: a thin carbon layer on a ceramic rod, with a spiral groove cut to set the value. Cheap, general purpose.
    • Metal film: a thin metal alloy layer instead of carbon. More accurate (typically ±1%), more stable with temperature and quieter, so preferred in audio and measurement circuits such as audiometer attenuators and microphone preamplifiers.
    • Wire-wound: resistance wire wound on a former. Handles high power (several watts or more) but has some inductance, so it is used in power supplies, not in signal paths.
    • Surface-mount (SMD) chip resistors: tiny rectangular parts soldered directly onto a circuit board. Almost all modern hearing aids, audiometers and computers use these because of their very small size.

    Every resistor is described by four ratings: its resistance value, its tolerance (how far the actual value may be from the marked value, for example ±5%), its power rating (for example 0.25 W) and its temperature coefficient (how much the value changes per degree Celsius, often given in parts per million per °C). Values are made in standard series (E12, E24 and so on, described in IEC 60063), which is why you find 4.7 kΩ and 5.6 kΩ resistors but not 5 kΩ ones in the E12 series.

On a four-band resistor, the first two bands are digits, the third is the multiplier (the number of zeros) and the fourth, usually set a little apart, is the tolerance. Five-band resistors have three digit bands before the multiplier.

    Variable resistors: potentiometer, rheostat and trimmer

    A potentiometer ("pot") has a resistive track with a terminal at each end (usually called A and B) and a sliding contact, the wiper (W), that can be moved along the track by a rotary shaft or a slider. The resistance between A and B is fixed (for example 10 kΩ); what changes is how that total is split between A to W and W to B. Tracks are made of carbon, cermet (ceramic and metal mixture, stable and durable), conductive plastic (smooth and long-lived, common in quality audio faders) or wire (for power).

    Potentiometer (three terminals)

    • All three terminals used.
    • Works as an adjustable voltage divider.
    • Examples: volume control of a speech trainer or a clinic amplifier, the attenuator knob on older audiometers, the recording level fader on a mixer.

    Rheostat (two terminals)

    • Only one end and the wiper are used.
    • Works as an adjustable series resistance to control current.
    • Examples: dimming a lamp, setting a current in a test bench, adjusting the current of an older battery charger.

    A trimmer (trim pot or preset) is a small potentiometer adjusted with a screwdriver and then left alone. It is used to set up or calibrate a circuit once, for example to set the maximum output of an older analogue hearing aid, or to trim the output level of one channel of an audiometer during factory or service calibration. Trimmers are not designed for frequent turning; many are rated for only a few hundred adjustments.

    The voltage divider

    When a voltage Vin is applied across the whole track of a potentiometer, the track acts as two resistors in series: R1 from A to the wiper and R2 from the wiper to B. The same current flows through both, so the voltage is shared in proportion to the resistances.

    Figure 1.1a. A potentiometer used as a voltage divider. Turning the shaft moves the wiper, changing R1 and R2 while their sum stays equal to the track resistance.

    Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2) where R2 is the resistance between the wiper and the grounded end B, and R1 + R2 is the whole track resistance.

    Worked example: a 10 kΩ linear potentiometer is connected across 5 V and the wiper is set so that R1 = 5.3 kΩ and R2 = 4.7 kΩ. Then Vout = 5 × 4.7 / 10 = 2.35 V. A fixed divider made of 10 kΩ (top) and 4.7 kΩ (bottom) would give Vout = 5 × 4.7 / 14.7 = 1.60 V.

    Common errorThe divider formula assumes that the circuit connected to Vout draws almost no current. If the next stage has a low input resistance, it appears in parallel with R2 and pulls Vout down. This is called loading. A good rule is that the load resistance should be at least ten times R2. Amplifier inputs in audio equipment are designed with high input resistance for this reason.

    Linear and logarithmic (audio) taper

    The taper (or law) of a potentiometer is the way its resistance ratio changes as the shaft is turned.

    • Linear taper: the output fraction is directly proportional to rotation. At half rotation, Vout is half of Vin. Used where a quantity must change evenly, for example setting a reference voltage or a balance control on a bench instrument.
    • Logarithmic (audio) taper: the track is made so that resistance changes slowly at the start of rotation and quickly towards the end. A common practical law gives about 10% of the output at half rotation. The name "logarithmic" is approximate: real audio pots usually follow two or three straight-line segments that together imitate a logarithmic curve.
    • Reverse log taper changes quickly first and slowly later; it is used less often.

    Markings differ between manufacturers and regions. On many modern parts "A" denotes an audio (logarithmic) taper and "B" a linear taper, but older and some regional conventions differ, so always confirm on the datasheet or by measurement.

    Why volume controls use a logarithmic taper

    Human hearing responds to sound level on a roughly logarithmic scale. Equal steps in perceived loudness correspond to roughly equal steps in decibels, that is, to equal ratios of sound pressure, not equal differences. A change in voltage ratio expressed in decibels is 20 log10(ratio) (see 1.4). For a linear pot:

    • At 50% rotation the output is 0.5 of maximum, which is only 20 log10(0.5) = −6 dB.
    • At 10% rotation it is 0.1, which is −20 dB.
    • At 1% rotation it is 0.01, which is −40 dB.

    So on a linear volume control the top half of the knob changes loudness by only 6 dB, while almost all the useful range is crammed into the first small movement near zero. With an audio taper giving about 10% at mid rotation, the midpoint is already −20 dB, and the loudness change feels spread more evenly across the whole rotation. This is why the volume control on a body-worn hearing aid, a speech trainer or a clinic amplifier uses a logarithmic law. The same thinking is behind the audiometer hearing level dial, which steps in equal decibel increments (usually 5 dB, with 1 or 2 dB steps available on many instruments), not equal voltage increments.

    Digital potentiometers

    Mechanical potentiometers wear out, collect dust (causing crackle), are bulky and cannot be set by a computer. A digital potentiometer (digipot) is an integrated circuit that performs the same three-terminal job electronically. It is set by sending it a number.

    Figure 1.1b. Inside a digital potentiometer. The number held in the wiper register closes one electronic switch, connecting the wiper output to one tap on a chain of equal resistors between terminals A and B.

    How it works

    1. A long chain of equal small resistors (the resistor string or ladder) runs from terminal A to terminal B, exactly like the track of a mechanical pot cut into equal pieces.
    2. Each junction between resistors (a tap) connects through an electronic switch, made of MOSFET transistors (see 1.3), to a common wiper line W.
    3. A microcontroller sends a number to the chip over a serial bus. The two most common buses are I2C (two wires: clock and data, with several chips sharing the same pair by address) and SPI (three or four wires: clock, data in, data out and chip select). Some simple digipots use only up and down pulses.
    4. The number is stored in the wiper register. A decoder closes the one switch that matches this number, so the wiper connects to that tap.
    5. Many digipots also contain non-volatile memory (EEPROM) so that the setting is restored after power is removed. Volatile parts usually start at mid-scale or zero when switched on.

    The number of taps is the resolution. Parts with 32, 64, 128, 256 and 1024 positions are common. The step in resistance is the end-to-end resistance divided by the number of steps.

    R_WB = (D / N) × R_AB + R_W where D is the code (0 to N), N the number of steps (for example 255 for an 8-bit part), R_AB the end-to-end resistance and R_W the wiper switch resistance.

    Worked example: an 8-bit, 10 kΩ digital potentiometer has 256 positions and 255 steps. Step size = 10 000 / 255 ≈ 39.2 Ω. With code D = 128 and a wiper resistance of 50 Ω (typical values are some tens of ohms to about 100 Ω), R_WB ≈ (128 / 255) × 10 000 + 50 ≈ 5020 + 50 = 5070 Ω. A 7-bit, 10 kΩ part (127 steps) would have steps of about 78.7 Ω.

    Some digital potentiometers and "digital attenuator" or "programmable gain" chips made for audio have a logarithmic law built in, stepping in fixed decibel increments (for example 0.5 dB or 1 dB per step) instead of equal resistance steps, for the reason explained above.

    Uses in speech and hearing equipment

    • Hearing aids: older analogue hearing aids had tiny screwdriver trimmers for gain, tone and maximum output. Programmable analogue aids replaced these with digitally set controls adjusted from a computer. In today's fully digital aids, gain is mostly set inside the digital signal processor by multiplying the sampled signal by a number, which is the software equivalent of a digital potentiometer, while the user's volume rocker simply sends a new number.
    • Audiometer attenuators: the hearing level control must change the output in exact, repeatable decibel steps over a range of more than 100 dB. Traditional audiometers used precision switched resistor networks; modern ones combine digital attenuators, programmable-gain stages and the digital-to-analogue converter to do the same job under microcontroller control. The step accuracy of this chain is one of the things checked during calibration (see 5.9 Audiometer output calibration).
    • Trimming and calibration: in service mode, a technician can adjust the output of each transducer at each frequency to match the reference levels. In older units this meant turning trimmers; in newer ones calibration values are numbers stored in memory and applied through digital gain.
    • Speech trainers, group amplification systems and recording interfaces: digitally controlled volume allows remote controls, presets for different children or rooms, and computer control.
    Common errorA digital potentiometer is not a free-floating resistor. The voltages on its A, B and W pins must stay within the chip's supply rails, the current through the wiper is limited (often to a few milliamperes), and the end-to-end resistance tolerance is loose (commonly ±20%). It works well for setting ratios (voltage divider use) but poorly as an accurate absolute resistance.

    Capacitors

    Structure and capacitance

    A capacitor consists of two conducting plates separated by an insulating layer called the dielectric. Dielectric materials include air, vacuum, paper, plastic film, mica, ceramic and a thin layer of aluminium oxide or tantalum oxide (in electrolytic capacitors). When a voltage is connected, electrons are pushed onto one plate and pulled off the other, so one plate becomes negatively charged and the other positively charged by the same amount, Q. No charge passes through the dielectric itself.

    The capacitance C tells us how much charge is stored for each volt applied:

    Q = C × V C = ε A / d = ε0 εr A / d where Q is the charge in coulombs, V the voltage, C the capacitance in farads (F), A the area of overlap of the plates in m², d the distance between the plates in m, ε0 = 8.854 × 10^-12 F/m (the permittivity of free space) and εr the relative permittivity (dielectric constant) of the insulator.

    So capacitance is directly proportional to plate area and to the permittivity of the dielectric, and inversely proportional to the distance between the plates. Larger plates hold more charge; bringing the plates closer increases the attraction between the opposite charges and lets more charge be stored at the same voltage. Typical relative permittivities are about 1 for air, roughly 2 to 4 for paper and plastic films, about 5 to 7 for mica, around 8 to 10 for aluminium oxide, and from about 10 to several thousand for different ceramics.

    Worked example: two plates each 1 cm² (1 × 10-4 m²) are 0.1 mm (1 × 10-4 m) apart in air. C = 8.854 × 10-12 × 1 × 10-4 / 1 × 10-4 = 8.85 × 10-12 F = 8.85 pF. The farad is a very large unit, so practical values are written in microfarads (µF, 10-6 F), nanofarads (nF, 10-9 F) and picofarads (pF, 10-12 F).

    The energy stored in the electric field between the plates is E = ½ C V². A 10 µF capacitor charged to 12 V holds Q = 10 × 10-6 × 12 = 120 µC and stores ½ × 10 × 10-6 × 144 = 0.72 mJ.

    Charging, discharging and the RC time constant

    When a capacitor is connected to a DC supply through a resistor, it does not charge instantly. At the first instant the capacitor voltage is zero, so the full supply voltage appears across the resistor and the current is large (V/R). As charge builds up, the capacitor voltage rises, the voltage left across the resistor falls, and the current decreases. The capacitor stops charging when its voltage equals the supply voltage; the current is then zero. This gives an exponential curve.

    Figure 1.1c. Left: a parallel-plate capacitor. Right: charging a capacitor through a resistor, and the resulting exponential rise of capacitor voltage, reaching about 63% of the supply at one time constant.

    τ = R × C vC(t) = Vs (1 − e^(−t/τ)) (charging) vC(t) = V0 e^(−t/τ) (discharging) where τ (tau) is the time constant in seconds, R in ohms, C in farads, Vs the supply voltage, V0 the starting voltage and e ≈ 2.718.

    After one time constant the capacitor reaches 1 − e-1 ≈ 63.2% of its final voltage; after 5τ it reaches about 99.3%, which is usually taken as fully charged. When discharging, it falls to about 36.8% of its starting value after one τ.

    Worked example: R = 10 kΩ and C = 100 µF give τ = 10 000 × 100 × 10-6 = 1 s. Charging from a 5 V supply, the capacitor reaches about 3.16 V after 1 s and is practically fully charged (about 4.97 V) after 5 s.

    Capacitors and AC: capacitive reactance

    With DC, once a capacitor is charged no further current flows: a capacitor blocks DC. With AC, the voltage keeps reversing, so the capacitor keeps charging and discharging and current keeps flowing in the external circuit, even though no charge crosses the dielectric. The opposition a capacitor offers to AC is its capacitive reactance, XC, measured in ohms.

    Xc = 1 / (2 π f C) where Xc is in ohms, f is the frequency in Hz and C is in farads.

    Reactance falls as frequency rises and as capacitance increases. At f = 0 (DC) the reactance is infinite. In a capacitor the current leads the voltage by 90°, and ideally no energy is turned into heat: energy is stored and returned every cycle.

    Worked example: a 10 µF capacitor at 50 Hz has XC = 1 / (2 × π × 50 × 10 × 10-6) ≈ 318 Ω. At 1 kHz the same capacitor offers only about 15.9 Ω.

    Uses of capacitors

    • Coupling: a capacitor in series between two amplifier stages passes the AC speech signal and blocks the DC bias voltage of one stage from upsetting the next. Together with the input resistance of the next stage it forms a high-pass filter with a corner frequency fc = 1 / (2πRC); with R = 10 kΩ and C = 1 µF, fc ≈ 15.9 Hz, low enough to pass all speech. Too small a coupling capacitor cuts bass (see 1.4).
    • Bypass and decoupling: a capacitor connected from a supply pin to ground provides a local reservoir of charge and shorts high-frequency noise to ground.
    • Filtering and smoothing: large capacitors smooth the pulsating output of a rectifier in a DC power supply (1.5). With resistors and inductors, capacitors form low-pass, high-pass and band-pass filters that shape the frequency response of hearing aids and audiometers (Unit 5).
    • Timing: the RC time constant sets the speed of timers, oscillators and the attack and release times of simple analogue compressors.
    • Condenser microphone: the microphone itself is a capacitor. A thin, stretched diaphragm forms one plate and a fixed back plate forms the other. Sound pressure moves the diaphragm, changing d, so C changes. With a fixed charge on the plates (from a polarising voltage, or from a permanently charged electret layer), V = Q/C changes in step with the sound. For an idealised 12 mm diameter diaphragm 20 µm from its back plate in air, C = 8.854 × 10-12 × 1.131 × 10-4 / 20 × 10-6 ≈ 50 pF; real capsules are typically in the range of some picofarads to some tens of picofarads. Measurement microphones in sound level meters and audiometer calibration systems are condenser microphones; almost all hearing-aid microphones are electret types (see 2.9 Microphones).

    Clinical applications

    • Checking a hearing aid with a weak or intermittent output: a worn volume control, or a trimmer that has moved, is a classic fault in older analogue aids, recognised by crackling when the control is turned (see Rout and Rajendran on troubleshooting).
    • Understanding why an audiometer's hearing level dial moves in equal decibel steps, and why its attenuator accuracy is checked at calibration.
    • Setting recording levels for speech analysis: a logarithmic gain control on the microphone preamplifier gives fine control near normal speaking levels.
    • Recognising that the measurement microphone of a sound level meter is a precision capacitor whose sensitivity depends on a very small, stable gap and, for externally polarised types, on a stable polarising voltage.
    • Explaining hum from old equipment: dried-out electrolytic smoothing capacitors let ripple into the audio signal.

    Demonstration

    Equipment: assorted resistors, a multimeter, a 10 kΩ linear and a 10 kΩ audio-taper potentiometer, a 5 V bench supply or battery, a breadboard, a 100 µF capacitor with a 10 kΩ resistor, an LED with a series resistor, an old analogue hearing aid with trimmers (if available), a digital potentiometer evaluation board or photographs of one.

    1. Read the colour code of several resistors and confirm each value with the multimeter. Compare with the tolerance band.
    2. Connect the linear pot across 5 V and measure the wiper voltage at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% rotation. Repeat with the audio-taper pot. Students plot both curves.
    3. Convert each reading to decibels relative to 5 V and show that the log pot gives more evenly spaced decibel steps.
    4. Connect a 10 kΩ load across the wiper and ground and show the loading effect on the linear pot.
    5. Charge the 100 µF capacitor through 10 kΩ while measuring the voltage every second; students observe the 63% point at about 1 s. Discharge it through the LED and its resistor and watch the light fade.
    6. Show the trimmers on an old hearing aid, and explain which parameter each set.
    7. Use the interactive demos above to repeat the taper and RC experiments with other values.

    Current developments

    • Mechanical controls are steadily being replaced by push buttons, touch controls and smartphone apps that send numbers to digital gain stages.
    • Digital potentiometers with non-volatile memory, finer resolution and built-in decibel laws are used for calibration trimming, so that instruments can be calibrated through software.
    • Resistors and capacitors continue to shrink; very small surface-mount packages make multi-microphone hearing aids and in-the-canal devices possible.
    • MEMS microphones, which are tiny capacitive sensors made on silicon chips, are now used widely in phones and increasingly in hearing devices.

    Inductors and transformers

    B.Sc. B2.42 hoursUnit 1

    An electric current always produces a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field always produces a voltage. Inductors and transformers are built on these two facts. This topic explains electromagnetic induction from the beginning, then shows how inductors store energy and oppose changes in current, and how transformers change AC voltages, isolate patients from the mains and match audio equipment. The same physics explains the hearing-aid telecoil and the hum picked up by electrophysiology leads.

    What it is

    An inductor is a coil of insulated wire, often wound on a core of iron or ferrite, that opposes any change in the current through it. A transformer is two (or more) coils wound on a shared core, so that an alternating current in one coil induces an alternating voltage in the other, with no electrical connection between them.

    Why it is required

    Mains power arrives at 230 V AC but electronic circuits need a few volts; a transformer steps the voltage down. Patient-connected equipment must be electrically separated from the mains; an isolation transformer provides that separation. Inductors smooth power supplies, block interference and form filters and loudspeaker crossovers. The telecoil in a hearing aid is an inductor that picks up sound sent as a magnetic field by a loop system.

    How it works

    Current in a coil creates magnetic flux. If the current changes, the flux changes, and by Faraday's law a voltage is induced; by Lenz's law this voltage opposes the change. In a single coil this self-induction makes the inductor resist changes of current. When a second coil shares the flux, the changing flux induces a voltage in it too, in proportion to its number of turns: this is mutual induction, the principle of the transformer.

    Background: magnetism from current

    When current flows in a wire, a magnetic field forms in circles around it. Winding the wire into a coil (a solenoid) makes the fields of all the turns add up inside the coil, producing a strong field along its axis, much like a bar magnet with a north and a south pole. The total amount of magnetic field passing through the coil is the magnetic flux, Φ (phi), measured in webers (Wb).

    Placing a core of iron or ferrite inside the coil greatly increases the flux for the same current, because these materials are easily magnetised. How easily a material is magnetised is its permeability, μ = μ0μr, where μ0 = 4π × 10-7 H/m is the permeability of free space and μr is the relative permeability (1 for air, typically hundreds to thousands for ferrites and silicon steel).

    Faraday's law and Lenz's law

    In 1831 Michael Faraday found that a voltage (an electromotive force, EMF) is induced in a coil whenever the magnetic flux linking it changes. It does not matter whether the change is caused by moving a magnet, moving the coil, or changing the current in a nearby coil. A steady flux, however strong, induces nothing.

    e = − N × dΦ/dt where e is the induced EMF in volts, N the number of turns, and dΦ/dt the rate of change of flux in webers per second.

    The minus sign expresses Lenz's law (after Heinrich Lenz): the induced EMF always acts in the direction that opposes the change that produced it. If the current in a coil is increasing, the induced EMF pushes back against the increase; if it is decreasing, the induced EMF tries to keep it flowing. Lenz's law is a consequence of conservation of energy.

    Inductors

    Self-inductance

    A single coil carrying a changing current experiences its own changing flux, so an EMF is induced in the coil itself. This is self-induction, and the induced EMF is often called the back EMF. The property of a coil that measures how much back EMF appears for a given rate of change of current is its inductance, L, measured in henries (H).

    v = L × di/dt where v is the voltage across the inductor, L the inductance in henries and di/dt the rate of change of current in amperes per second.

    A coil has an inductance of one henry when a current changing at one ampere per second induces one volt in it. Practical values range from nanohenries (nH) in radio circuits through microhenries (µH) and millihenries (mH) in audio and filter work to several henries in older mains smoothing chokes. For a long coil:

    L = μ0 μr N² A / l where N is the number of turns, A the cross-sectional area of the coil in m², l its length in m. Inductance rises with the square of the number of turns.

    Worked example: a coil of 100 turns, 1 cm² cross-section and 2 cm long, in air: L = 4π × 10-7 × 100² × 1 × 10-4 / 0.02 ≈ 62.8 µH. With an ideal ferrite core of μr = 1000 filling the coil, L would be about 62.8 mH. Doubling the turns to 200 multiplies L by four.

    Worked example: a 10 mH inductor carries a current that changes by 0.2 A in 1 ms. The back EMF is v = 0.01 × 0.2 / 0.001 = 2 V.

    Energy storage

    Building up the current in an inductor requires work against the back EMF. That work is stored in the magnetic field:

    E = ½ L I² where E is the stored energy in joules and I the current in amperes.

    A 10 mH inductor carrying 0.5 A stores ½ × 0.01 × 0.25 = 1.25 mJ. The inductance itself is a fixed property of the coil; what builds up and settles is the current and its magnetic field. When the current is reduced, the field collapses and the stored energy is returned to the circuit as electrical energy. If the current is interrupted suddenly, for example by opening a switch in series with a relay coil, di/dt becomes very large and the back EMF can reach hundreds of volts, causing a spark at the switch or destroying a transistor. This is why a diode is placed across relay coils (a flyback diode, see 1.3).

    Capacitor

    • Stores energy in an electric field, ½CV².
    • Opposes sudden changes of voltage.
    • Blocks DC, passes high frequencies.
    • Xc = 1/(2πfC) falls with frequency.
    • Time constant τ = RC.

    Inductor

    • Stores energy in a magnetic field, ½LI².
    • Opposes sudden changes of current.
    • Passes DC, opposes high frequencies.
    • XL = 2πfL rises with frequency.
    • Time constant τ = L/R.

    Inductors and AC: inductive reactance

    With AC the current is always changing, so the inductor always produces a back EMF that opposes the current. The faster the change (the higher the frequency) and the larger the inductance, the greater the opposition. This opposition is the inductive reactance:

    XL = 2 π f L where XL is in ohms, f the frequency in Hz and L the inductance in henries.

    At DC (f = 0) the reactance is zero, and only the small resistance of the copper wire remains. In an ideal inductor the current lags the voltage by 90°, and energy is stored and returned each cycle with no heat. When a coil is connected to DC through a resistance R, the current rises exponentially with time constant τ = L/R: a 10 mH coil with 10 Ω of resistance gives τ = 1 ms.

    Inductors in speech and hearing equipment

    • Telecoil (T-coil): a tiny coil of very fine wire on a ferrite rod inside a hearing aid or cochlear implant processor. An induction loop system (a wire around a room, driven by a loop amplifier) or a hearing-aid-compatible telephone produces a magnetic field that varies with the speech signal. By Faraday's law this field induces a small voltage in the telecoil, which the hearing aid amplifies. The listener receives the talker's voice directly, free of room noise and reverberation. Pickup is best when the coil axis is aligned with the field, which is why head position matters.
    • Moving-coil transducers: the voice coil of a loudspeaker or dynamic microphone is an inductor moving in a magnetic field (2.10 Loudspeakers and earphones). Its inductance makes the impedance of a loudspeaker rise at high frequencies.
    • Filters: inductors combine with capacitors in power-supply filters and in passive loudspeaker crossover networks that send low frequencies to a woofer and high frequencies to a tweeter.
    • Interference: the same law means that any loop of wire near a changing magnetic field (for example a mains transformer or fluorescent light ballast) picks up hum. ABR and EEG electrode leads should be kept short and twisted together, and away from transformers, to reduce the loop area.

    Transformers

    Principle: mutual induction

    A transformer is a static device (no moving parts) that transfers AC electrical energy from one circuit to another by mutual induction, with no direct electrical connection. The coil connected to the source is the primary winding; the coil connected to the load is the secondary winding. Both are wound on the same closed magnetic core.

    1. An alternating voltage applied to the primary drives an alternating current through it.
    2. This current sets up an alternating magnetic flux in the core. The core provides an easy (low-reluctance) path, so almost all of the flux links the secondary as well.
    3. The changing flux induces an EMF in every turn of both windings. The EMF per turn is the same in both, so the voltage of each winding is proportional to its number of turns.
    4. If a load is connected to the secondary, current flows and power is delivered. The frequency of the output is the same as that of the input; only voltage and current levels change.

    Figure 1.2a. A simple transformer. The primary (six turns drawn) and the secondary (three turns drawn) share the flux in a closed laminated core, so this sketch shows a 2:1 step-down transformer. The two windings are joined only magnetically.

    Turns ratio, voltage and current

    Vs / Vp = Ns / Np Is / Ip = Np / Ns Vp × Ip = Vs × Is (ideal transformer) where Vp, Vs are the primary and secondary voltages (RMS), Ip, Is the currents, and Np, Ns the numbers of turns.
    • If Ns < Np, the secondary voltage is lower: a step-down transformer (for example 230 V to 12 V in a power supply).
    • If Ns > Np, the secondary voltage is higher: a step-up transformer (for example in a UPS inverter, 1.7).
    • If Ns = Np (1:1), the voltage is unchanged: an isolation transformer.

    Power is conserved (apart from losses), so whatever happens to voltage happens inversely to current. A step-down transformer delivers a larger current at a lower voltage than it draws from the mains.

    Worked example: a mains transformer for an audiometer power supply converts 230 V to 12 V. The primary has 1150 turns. Ns = Np × Vs / Vp = 1150 × 12 / 230 = 60 turns. If the secondary supplies 1 A, the ideal primary current is 1 × 12 / 230 ≈ 0.052 A (52 mA). If the transformer is rated 24 VA, the maximum secondary current is 24 / 12 = 2 A and the primary current at full load is about 24 / 230 ≈ 0.104 A.

    Why a transformer works only on AC

    Induction needs a changing flux. A steady DC current produces a steady flux, so nothing is induced in the secondary. Worse, the primary then offers only its small wire resistance (there is no inductive reactance at DC), so a large current flows and the winding overheats. Transformers therefore must never be connected to DC. This is also why a UPS or inverter must first chop battery DC into AC before a transformer can step it up.

    Impedance matching

    A transformer also transforms impedance. A load Zs on the secondary appears to the source on the primary as a larger or smaller impedance:

    Zp = Zs × (Np / Ns)² where Zp is the impedance seen at the primary and Zs the load impedance on the secondary.

    Worked example: an amplifier designed to drive 800 Ω must feed an 8 Ω loudspeaker. Required impedance ratio = 800 / 8 = 100, so the turns ratio is √100 = 10, that is, 10:1. Audio matching transformers of this kind were used in valve (vacuum tube) amplifiers, in public-address systems that run long speaker lines at higher voltage, and in microphone inputs to match and balance low-impedance microphones.

    Construction

    • Core: for mains frequencies, thin sheets (laminations) of silicon steel, insulated from one another and stacked, often in E and I shapes. For high frequencies (switch-mode supplies, chargers) the core is ferrite. Toroidal (ring) cores give low stray field and are used where hum pickup matters.
    • Windings: enamelled copper wire, the low-voltage winding usually thicker because it carries more current. Windings are often placed on a plastic former (bobbin), sometimes split into two sections for better insulation between primary and secondary.
    • Insulation: layers of paper, polyester film or tape between layers and between windings. Medical and isolation transformers use reinforced insulation tested at high voltage.
    • Screen: an earthed copper foil between primary and secondary, in some designs, reduces capacitive coupling of electrical noise (electromagnetic interference) from the mains to the secondary.
    • Core-type and shell-type: in a core-type transformer the windings surround the limbs of the core; in a shell-type transformer the core surrounds the windings, with both windings on the central limb.

    Isolation transformers in the clinic

    Figure 1.2b. An isolation transformer supplying patient-connected equipment. Energy crosses the transformer magnetically, while the secondary circuit is not referenced to the mains conductors.

    Because the secondary of an isolation transformer is not connected to the mains live or neutral, a person touching one secondary conductor and earth does not complete a circuit back to the supply. It also reduces the leakage currents that can flow through a patient when several mains-powered devices (an ABR system, its computer, a printer) are connected together near the patient, and it blocks some electrical noise. A dedicated medical isolation transformer is built and tested to medical safety standards; an ordinary transformer or an autotransformer is not a substitute. How isolation transformers fit into the clinic's earthing and power arrangements is covered in 1.6 AC power supply, earthing and isolation transformers.

    Clinical applications

    • The linear power supply of many audiometers, amplifiers and speech trainers begins with a mains step-down transformer (1.5).
    • Chargers for hearing aids, cochlear implant processors and phones use small high-frequency ferrite transformers inside switch-mode supplies; wireless (inductive) chargers use two separated coils that behave as a loosely coupled transformer.
    • Isolation transformers supply electrophysiology systems (ABR, ASSR, OAE with a PC) in the patient environment, improving safety and reducing hum.

    • Stray magnetic fields from transformers, UPS units and fluorescent fittings are a common source of 50 Hz hum in ABR recordings and in telecoil listening.

    Diodes and transistors

    B.Sc. B2.41.5 hoursUnit 1Supporting topic

    Diodes and transistors are the semiconductor devices that turn passive components into working electronics. A diode lets current flow in one direction only, which is how mains AC is turned into DC for instruments. A transistor lets a small current or voltage control a much larger one, which is how signals are amplified and switched. This supporting topic gives the background needed for power supplies (1.5) and amplifiers (1.4).

    What it is

    A diode is a two-terminal semiconductor device made from a single PN junction; it conducts easily in one direction and blocks current in the other. A transistor is a three-terminal semiconductor device in which the current or voltage at one terminal controls the current between the other two. The two main families are the bipolar junction transistor (BJT) and the field-effect transistor (FET, including the MOSFET).

    Why it is required

    Every mains-powered audiometer, amplifier and charger needs diodes to rectify AC into DC. Every hearing aid, audiometer and computer depends on transistors: a hearing-aid processor chip contains a very large number of them, the electret microphone in the aid has one built in, and the output stage that drives the receiver is made of transistors. LEDs, a kind of diode, light indicators, otoscopes and displays.

    How it works

    Pure silicon is a poor conductor. Adding tiny amounts of impurity (doping) creates N-type material with spare electrons or P-type material with "holes" (missing electrons). Where P and N meet, a thin depletion region with a built-in barrier forms. A voltage in one direction lowers this barrier and current flows; in the other direction it raises the barrier and current stops. In a transistor, a thin middle layer lets a small base current or gate voltage control a large flow of carriers between the outer layers.

    Background: semiconductors and doping

    Materials can be grouped by how easily they conduct. Conductors (copper, aluminium) have many free electrons. Insulators (glass, plastic, rubber) have almost none. Semiconductors such as silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge) lie in between: at room temperature they have only a few free carriers, but their conductivity can be changed enormously and precisely.

    Each silicon atom has four outer (valence) electrons, which it shares with four neighbours in a crystal. Pure silicon is called intrinsic. Doping means adding a very small, controlled amount of another element:

    • N-type: adding a pentavalent element (five valence electrons), such as phosphorus, leaves one spare electron per impurity atom free to move. Electrons are the majority carriers.
    • P-type: adding a trivalent element (three valence electrons), such as boron, leaves a missing electron, a hole, which behaves like a positive charge that can move as neighbouring electrons fill it. Holes are the majority carriers.

    Both types are still electrically neutral overall; they simply have different kinds of mobile charge. A small number of carriers of the opposite type (minority carriers) is always present because heat breaks some bonds.

    The PN junction diode

    When P-type and N-type regions are formed in one crystal, electrons near the junction diffuse from N to P and fill holes, and holes diffuse from P to N. This leaves a thin zone on each side of the junction with no mobile carriers, only fixed charged ions: the depletion region. These ions create an electric field and a barrier potential (about 0.6 to 0.7 V for silicon and about 0.2 to 0.3 V for germanium at room temperature) that stops further diffusion.

    Figure 1.3a. A PN junction diode. Top: the P and N regions with the depletion region between them. Bottom: the circuit symbol; the bar marks the cathode, which is also shown by a band printed on the body of a real diode.

    Forward and reverse bias

    Forward bias

    • Anode connected to the positive side, cathode to the negative side.
    • The applied voltage opposes the barrier, the depletion region narrows, and majority carriers cross the junction.
    • Very little current flows until the applied voltage approaches the barrier potential; then current rises steeply.
    • Current must be limited by a series resistance, or the diode overheats.

    Reverse bias

    • Anode connected to the negative side, cathode to the positive side.
    • The applied voltage adds to the barrier, the depletion region widens, and majority carriers cannot cross.
    • Only a tiny reverse saturation (leakage) current of minority carriers flows, typically nanoamperes for small silicon diodes, rising with temperature.
    • At a large enough reverse voltage, breakdown occurs and current rises sharply.

    The V-I characteristic

    The graph of diode current against voltage is its V-I characteristic. It is measured with the diode, a series current-limiting resistor, a variable DC supply, an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the diode.

    • Forward region: current stays very small up to the knee or cut-in voltage (about 0.6 to 0.7 V for silicon, 0.2 to 0.3 V for germanium), then rises almost exponentially. Once conducting, a silicon diode's voltage stays close to 0.7 V over a wide range of current. If the current exceeds the rated maximum, the diode overheats and is destroyed.
    • Reverse region: a tiny, nearly constant leakage current flows until the breakdown voltage. Beyond it the current rises very quickly. An ordinary rectifier diode is not designed to work in breakdown; the large current and power there usually destroy it. A Zener diode is designed to work there.
    I = Is × (e^(V / (n VT)) − 1) the Shockley diode equation, where Is is the saturation current, n an ideality factor (about 1 to 2), VT = kT/q ≈ 25.9 mV at 300 K the thermal voltage.

    Two useful consequences: for a silicon diode each tenfold increase of current raises the forward voltage by only about 60 mV to 120 mV (n VT ln 10), and the forward voltage at fixed current falls by roughly 2 mV for every degree Celsius rise in temperature.

    • Zener diode: heavily doped so that breakdown occurs at a precise, low reverse voltage (for example 3.3 V, 5.1 V, 12 V).     • Light-emitting diode (LED): in forward bias, electrons and holes recombine and release energy as light. LEDs are used as power and signal indicators on audiometers, as light sources in otoscopes and video otoscopes, and in displays.
    • Photodiode: a reverse-biased junction whose leakage current increases with light; used in light sensors and optical links.
    • Schottky diode: a metal to semiconductor junction with a low forward drop and fast switching; common in switch-mode power supplies.

    Uses of diodes

    • Rectification: converting AC to DC in power supplies and battery chargers (half-wave, full-wave, bridge; see 1.5).
    • Protection: a diode in series with a battery input prevents damage if the battery is inserted the wrong way; a flyback diode across a relay coil absorbs the back EMF when the coil is switched off (1.2); clamp diodes at amplifier inputs limit harmful voltages.
    • Signal processing: detecting the envelope of a signal, clipping peaks, and switching signals.
    • Voltage reference: Zener diodes and similar reference devices.

    The bipolar junction transistor (BJT)

    Construction

    A BJT is a single crystal with three doped regions forming two PN junctions. In an NPN transistor a thin P-type layer is sandwiched between two N-type regions; in a PNP transistor a thin N-type layer lies between two P-type regions. The three regions are:

    • Emitter (E): heavily doped, so that it can inject (emit) a large number of majority carriers into the base: electrons in an NPN transistor, holes in a PNP transistor.
    • Base (B): very thin and lightly doped, so that most carriers from the emitter pass straight through it instead of recombining there.
    • Collector (C): moderately doped and physically the largest, because it collects the carriers and dissipates most of the heat.

    Because the emitter and collector differ in doping and size, they are not interchangeable. In the circuit symbol, the arrow is on the emitter and points in the direction of conventional current: outwards for NPN, inwards for PNP.

    Common errorA transistor is sometimes described as "two diodes back to back". That picture helps when testing a transistor with a multimeter, because each junction behaves like a diode. It does not explain amplification: two separate diodes wired together do not make a transistor. Transistor action depends on the base being extremely thin and inside one crystal, so that carriers injected by the emitter reach the collector.

    Figure 1.3b. Left: the three regions of an NPN transistor. Right: the transistor used as a switch; a small base current from a microcontroller pin switches a much larger collector current through a relay coil or LED.

    How a BJT works

    In normal amplifying use (the active region), the base-emitter junction is forward biased (about 0.7 V for silicon) and the collector-base junction is reverse biased. Electrons from the emitter of an NPN transistor flood into the thin base. Only a few (typically around 1%) recombine there and leave as base current; the rest are swept across into the collector by the field of the reverse-biased collector junction. The result is that a small base current controls a collector current many times larger.

    IC = β × IB IE = IB + IC where IB, IC, IE are the base, collector and emitter currents, and β (also written hFE) is the DC current gain, typically about 50 to 300 for small transistors and varying widely even between samples of the same type.

    Worked example: with β = 100 and IB = 20 µA, IC = 100 × 20 µA = 2 mA and IE = 2.02 mA.

    Transistor as a switch

    A microcontroller pin can supply only a few milliamperes, not enough to drive a relay, a motor or a bright LED. A transistor solves this: with the pin at 0 V the transistor is cut off (load OFF); with the pin at 5 V enough base current flows to saturate it (load ON). The base resistor limits the base current.

    Worked example: a relay coil needs 50 mA and the transistor's minimum β is 50. The minimum base current is 50 mA / 50 = 1 mA; designers usually supply about twice this to ensure saturation, 2 mA. From a 5 V pin, RB = (5 − 0.7) / 0.002 = 2150 Ω, so a standard 2.2 kΩ resistor is used. A flyback diode is placed across the relay coil.

    Transistor as an amplifier

    In the common-emitter amplifier, the transistor is biased (by resistors) into the middle of the active region. A small AC signal, for example from a microphone, is added to the base through a coupling capacitor. It varies the base current slightly; the collector current varies β times as much; flowing through a collector resistor RC, this produces a much larger voltage variation at the collector. The output is inverted (180° phase shift): when the input rises, the collector voltage falls. With an emitter resistor RE included, the voltage gain is approximately −RC/RE, which is stable because it depends on resistors rather than on β. For RC = 4.7 kΩ and RE = 470 Ω, the gain is about −10. Amplifiers are developed further in 1.4.

    Field-effect transistors and the MOSFET

    A field-effect transistor (FET) controls current with a voltage instead of a current. Its terminals are the gate (G), source (S) and drain (D). In a MOSFET (metal oxide semiconductor FET), the gate is separated from the silicon by a very thin insulating oxide layer. A voltage on the gate creates an electric field that forms (or removes) a conducting channel between source and drain.

    • The gate draws almost no steady current, so the input resistance is extremely high.
    • MOSFETs make excellent switches, and pairs of complementary MOSFETs form CMOS logic, the technology of microcontrollers, memory and the digital signal processors inside hearing aids and cochlear implant processors.
    • Power MOSFETs switch large currents in switch-mode power supplies, UPS inverters and class D amplifiers.
    • The electret microphone capsule contains a small JFET (junction FET) that buffers the very high-impedance capsule; this is why electret microphones need a small bias voltage.
    • The thin gate oxide can be destroyed by static electricity, so MOSFET devices and hearing-aid circuit boards are handled with anti-static precautions.

    BJT

    • Current-controlled (base current).
    • Lower input resistance.
    • Low noise at low source impedance; common in discrete audio stages.
    • Needs about 0.7 V at the base to turn on.

    MOSFET

    • Voltage-controlled (gate voltage).
    • Extremely high input resistance.
    • Dominant in integrated circuits and power switching.
    • Sensitive to static discharge.

    Key points to remember

    • N-type: electrons are majority carriers; P-type: holes are majority carriers.
    • A PN junction has a depletion region and a barrier potential of about 0.7 V (Si) or 0.3 V (Ge).
    • Forward bias: conducts above the knee voltage; reverse bias: only tiny leakage until breakdown.

    • BJT: emitter heavily doped, base thin and lightly doped, collector largest; IC = βIB, IE = IB + IC.
    • Switch: cut-off (OFF) and saturation (ON); amplifier: active region.
    • MOSFET: voltage-controlled, very high input resistance, the building block of digital chips.

    Amplifiers: concept of gain, frequency response and bandwidth

    B.Sc. B2.42 hoursUnit 1

    An amplifier makes a weak signal stronger. The microphone of a hearing aid produces a few millivolts; the receiver needs much more. An audiometer must deliver tones at exact levels across a wide frequency range. This topic defines gain and expresses it in decibels, explains frequency response, bandwidth and the −3 dB points, introduces distortion and clipping, and shows how an operational amplifier sets gain with two resistors.

    What it is

    An amplifier is a circuit that produces an output signal with the same shape as its input signal but larger in voltage, current or power. The extra energy comes from the amplifier's power supply (battery or mains), not from the signal. The ratio of output to input is the gain.

    Why it is required

    Transducers produce tiny signals: a microphone gives millivolts, and the brainstem response picked up by ABR electrodes is well under a microvolt. These must be raised to levels that can drive earphones and loudspeakers, be converted to digital form, or be displayed. Amplifiers must do this over the right range of frequencies, with low noise and little distortion.

    How it works

    Transistors (1.3), usually inside an integrated circuit such as an operational amplifier, let the small input signal control a large flow of current from the power supply. Feedback resistors fix the gain precisely. Capacitors and the transistors' own limitations make the gain fall at very low and very high frequencies, so every amplifier has a finite bandwidth.

    Background: what an amplifier does

    Figure 1.4a. An amplifier takes a small input signal and produces a larger copy of it, drawing the extra energy from its power supply. The triangle is the usual symbol for an amplifier.

    Amplifiers are described by what they are designed to increase and by where they sit in a system:

    • Pre-amplifier (preamp): the first stage after a transducer. It must add as little noise as possible, because any noise it adds is amplified by every later stage. Example: the microphone preamplifier in a hearing aid or a speech recording interface, the head-stage of an ABR system.
    • Voltage amplifier: raises signal voltage between stages, often with filtering and gain control.
    • Power amplifier: the final stage that delivers enough current and power to drive a low-impedance transducer: an earphone, a bone vibrator, a loudspeaker, or a loop.
    • Differential or instrumentation amplifier: amplifies the difference between two inputs and rejects signals common to both. Essential for electrophysiology (ABR, ASSR), where the tiny neural signal is the difference between two electrodes while mains hum appears on both.
    • Buffer: a gain of 1 but with high input resistance and low output resistance, used to prevent loading (see the voltage divider in 1.1).

    Pre-amplifiers, power amplifiers and filters as common building blocks of instruments are studied in more detail in Unit 5.

    The concept of gain

    Voltage gain Av = Vout / Vin Current gain Ai = Iout / Iin Power gain Ap = Pout / Pin where all quantities are measured the same way (for example all RMS values). Gains are ratios and have no units.

    A gain greater than 1 means amplification; a gain less than 1 means attenuation (the signal is made smaller), as in a volume control or an audiometer attenuator. Worked example: 10 mV in and 1 V out gives Av = 1 / 0.01 = 100.

    Gain in decibels

    Gains in audio systems cover huge ranges, and hearing judges level on a logarithmic scale, so gain is usually expressed in decibels (dB). The decibel is based on a power ratio. Because power is proportional to voltage squared (P = V²/R, 1.1), voltage and current ratios use a factor 20 instead of 10.

The same arithmetic is used in audiometers, where the oscillator level, attenuator setting and output amplifier gain combine to give the dial reading in dB HL.

    Frequency response and bandwidth

    No amplifier has the same gain at all frequencies. The frequency response is the graph of gain (in dB) against frequency (on a logarithmic axis, so that each octave or decade takes the same space, just as on an audiogram). A typical response has three parts:

    • A flat midband region where the gain is at its designed value.
    • A low-frequency roll-off: coupling and bypass capacitors (1.1) have high reactance at low frequencies and form high-pass filters, so the gain falls. Amplifiers that are DC-coupled have no low-frequency roll-off.
    • A high-frequency roll-off: small internal capacitances of transistors and wiring, the limited speed of the amplifier, and deliberate low-pass filters make the gain fall at high frequencies.

    Figure 1.4c. Frequency response of an amplifier with lower cut-off fL = 100 Hz and upper cut-off fH = 10 kHz. The bandwidth lies between the two −3 dB points.

    The −3 dB points and bandwidth

    The edges of the useful range are defined where the gain has fallen by 3 dB from its midband value. At these −3 dB points (also called cut-off, corner or half-power frequencies) the output voltage is 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 of its midband value and the output power is half. The lower one is fL, the upper one fH.

    Bandwidth BW = fH − fL fc = 1 / (2 π R C) (simple RC corner) where fH and fL are the upper and lower −3 dB frequencies in Hz. For a single RC network, fc is its −3 dB frequency.

    Worked example: an amplifier has fL = 100 Hz and fH = 10 kHz. BW = 10 000 − 100 = 9900 Hz. If the midband gain is 40 dB, the gain at 100 Hz and at 10 kHz is 37 dB. Beyond a single-RC corner the gain falls by about 6 dB per octave (20 dB per decade); steeper filters (second order and above) fall at 12 dB per octave or more.

    Gain-bandwidth product

    Many amplifiers, in particular the operational amplifiers described below, trade gain against bandwidth. For a typical general-purpose op-amp the product of closed-loop gain and upper cut-off frequency is roughly constant: the gain-bandwidth product (GBW).

    fH ≈ GBW / Av where Av is the closed-loop voltage gain (as a ratio) and GBW the gain-bandwidth product in Hz, given in the op-amp datasheet.

    Worked example: an op-amp with GBW = 1 MHz set to a gain of 100 (40 dB) has fH ≈ 1 000 000 / 100 = 10 kHz, which is only just enough for audiometry. Splitting the gain into two stages of 10 each gives each stage fH ≈ 100 kHz; the pair together has an upper −3 dB point of about 64 kHz, far above the audio band. This is one reason why high overall gains are shared among several stages.

    Distortion, clipping and noise

    An ideal amplifier makes an exact, larger copy of the input. Real amplifiers change the waveform slightly, which is distortion.

    • Clipping: the output of an amplifier cannot swing beyond its power-supply voltages (the rails). If the input is so large that the output would need to exceed the rails, the peaks are cut flat. Worked example: an amplifier with a gain of 50 powered from ±9 V can accept at most about 9 / 50 = 0.18 V peak at its input; a 0.25 V peak input would need 12.5 V peak output and is clipped. The margin between the normal signal level and the clipping level is the headroom.
    • Harmonic distortion: a pure tone at the input comes out with added components at 2, 3, 4 ... times its frequency. It is expressed as total harmonic distortion (THD), the RMS of all harmonics as a percentage of the fundamental. Clipping produces large amounts of odd harmonics.
    • Intermodulation distortion: two tones together produce extra tones at sum and difference frequencies, making speech in noise sound rough.
    • Noise: random hiss added by resistors and transistors. It matters most in the first stage (the preamp), because it is amplified by every stage after.
    Clinical linkDistortion matters in audiology. A pure-tone audiometer must present tones with very low harmonic distortion; otherwise a patient with a steep high-frequency loss might respond to a harmonic instead of the test tone. Audiometer standards (IEC 60645-1) set limits on harmonic distortion, and hearing-aid measurement standards (IEC 60118-7, ANSI/ASA S3.22) include a THD test. A hearing aid driven into clipping by loud sounds sounds harsh; modern aids use compression and output limiting to avoid this.

    The operational amplifier

    An operational amplifier (op-amp) is an integrated circuit amplifier with two inputs, inverting (−) and non-inverting (+), and one output. By itself it has an extremely high gain (typically 100 000 or more at DC), a very high input resistance and a low output resistance. It amplifies the difference between its two inputs. Used "open loop", this enormous gain would drive the output straight into the rails. Instead it is almost always used with negative feedback: part of the output is fed back to the inverting input through resistors. The feedback forces the two inputs to nearly the same voltage and makes the gain depend only on the resistor values, which are accurate and stable.

    Figure 1.4d. The two basic op-amp amplifiers. Left: inverting. Right: non-inverting. In both, the feedback resistor Rf returns part of the output to the inverting input, and the gain is set by a ratio of resistors.

    Inverting: Av = − Rf / Rin Non-inverting: Av = 1 + Rf / Rg Voltage follower: Av = 1 (Rf = 0, Rg absent) The minus sign means the output is inverted (180° phase shift). The non-inverting amplifier keeps the phase and has a very high input resistance.

    Worked examples: an inverting amplifier with Rin = 10 kΩ and Rf = 100 kΩ has Av = −10 (20 dB); a 50 mV input gives −0.5 V. A non-inverting amplifier with Rg = 10 kΩ and Rf = 90 kΩ has Av = 1 + 9 = 10 (20 dB). For a microphone preamplifier needing 34 dB (×50), a non-inverting stage with Rg = 1 kΩ and Rf = 49 kΩ (in practice the nearby standard value 47 kΩ gives ×48, about 33.6 dB) would be used. Replacing Rf by a potentiometer or digital potentiometer gives an adjustable gain.

    Key points to remember

    • An amplifier makes a larger copy of a signal using energy from its power supply.
    • Gain = output / input; in dB, 10 log for power, 20 log for voltage or current.
    • ×2 voltage = 6 dB, ×10 = 20 dB, ×100 = 40 dB; ×0.707 = −3 dB.
    • Gains of cascaded stages add in dB.
    • Frequency response: gain against frequency; bandwidth = fH − fL between the −3 dB (half-power) points.
    • A single RC corner is at 1/(2πRC) and rolls off at 6 dB per octave.
    • Op-amp: inverting gain −Rf/Rin; non-inverting gain 1 + Rf/Rg.
    • Gain-bandwidth product: higher gain per stage means lower fH.
    • Clipping occurs when the output would exceed the supply rails; it creates harmonic distortion.
    • The first stage should have the lowest noise, because its noise is amplified by all later stages.

    DC power supply: block diagram, rectifiers, filters, regulators, SMPS and batteries

    B.Sc. B2.43 hoursUnit 1

    Every audiometer, hearing aid, tympanometer and computer in a clinic runs on steady direct current (DC), while the wall socket delivers 230 V alternating current (AC). The DC power supply is the chain of transformer, rectifier, filter and regulator that makes this conversion, or the battery that replaces it in portable devices. A quiet, stable supply is the first requirement for accurate, noise-free measurements.

    What it is

    A circuit that takes the 230 V, 50 Hz AC mains (or a battery) and delivers a fixed, smooth DC voltage such as 5 V, 12 V or 24 V to electronic circuits. It is either a linear power supply or a switched-mode power supply (SMPS).

    Why it is required

    Transistors, integrated circuits, microphones' preamplifiers and processors only work correctly from a steady DC voltage of the right value. Mains voltage is far too high, it reverses direction 100 times a second and it fluctuates. Any ripple or noise on the supply can appear as hum in a hearing test signal.

    How it works

    A transformer reduces the voltage, diodes in a rectifier allow current in one direction only, capacitors and inductors in a filter smooth out the pulses, and a regulator holds the output constant when the mains or the load changes. An SMPS does the same job by switching at high frequency with a much smaller transformer.

    Background: AC, DC and what a power supply must do

    Direct current (DC) flows in one direction only and, ideally, has a constant value: the output of a battery is the everyday example. Alternating current (AC) reverses direction periodically. The Indian mains supply is a sine wave of 230 V RMS at 50 Hz, which means it rises to about +325 V, falls through zero to about −325 V and returns, fifty times every second (topic 1.6 explains RMS and peak values).

    Electronic circuits need DC for a simple reason. A transistor amplifier is set up (biased) so that it sits at a steady operating point, and the tiny speech or tone signal is added on top of that point. If the supply itself swings up and down, the operating point moves and the output contains the supply variation. In an audiometer this appears as a 50 Hz or 100 Hz hum under the test tone; in a digital circuit a supply that dips too low can reset the processor.

    A power supply therefore has four jobs, which also give the classic block diagram:

    1. Change the voltage level (230 V down to, say, 12 V). This is done by a transformer.
    2. Make the current flow in one direction only. This is rectification, done by diodes.
    3. Smooth the resulting pulses into nearly steady DC. This is filtering, done mainly by capacitors.
    4. Hold the output at an exact value despite changes in mains voltage or load current. This is regulation.

    Some useful terms, defined once here:

    • Load: whatever the supply powers (a circuit board, a hearing-aid amplifier). It draws a load current IL.
    • Ripple: the small AC variation left on top of the DC output after filtering, measured peak to peak (Vr(p-p)) or as an RMS value.
    • Ripple factor: ripple RMS voltage divided by the DC (average) voltage. Smaller is better.
    • Regulation: how well the output stays constant. Line regulation refers to changes in input (mains) voltage; load regulation refers to changes in load current.
    • Efficiency: output power divided by input power, as a percentage. The rest becomes heat.

    Block diagram of a regulated DC power supply

    Figure 1.5a. Block diagram of a regulated linear DC power supply. The small sketches show the voltage waveform after each stage (not to scale).

    Follow the signal from left to right:

    1. Transformer: the 230 V RMS mains (325 V peak) is stepped down to a lower AC voltage, for example 12 V RMS (about 17 V peak). The transformer also separates (isolates) the low-voltage circuit from the mains wiring, which is an important safety feature.
    2. Rectifier: one, two or four diodes convert the AC into pulsating DC: a series of positive humps. The voltage is now always positive, but it still falls to zero between humps.
    3. Filter: a large capacitor (sometimes with an inductor) charges up at each peak and supplies the load between peaks, so the output becomes DC with a small sawtooth ripple.
    4. Regulator: a Zener diode or a regulator IC removes the remaining ripple and keeps the output at a fixed value (for example 5 V or 12 V) even if the mains changes from 210 V to 250 V or the load current changes.
    5. Load: the circuit being powered, which now sees a steady DC voltage.

    The transformer

    A transformer transfers electrical energy from one circuit to another through a shared magnetic field, with no direct wire connection between the two. It has two coils of insulated copper wire, the primary winding (connected to the supply) and the secondary winding (connected to the load), wound on a common core of laminated silicon steel.

    Principle. An alternating current in the primary produces an alternating magnetic flux in the core. The core guides almost all of this flux through the secondary coil. By Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction (Michael Faraday, 1831), a changing flux linking a coil induces a voltage in it, proportional to the number of turns and to the rate of change of flux. The same flux links both windings, so each turn of wire has the same induced voltage, and the winding voltages are in the ratio of their turns. By Lenz's law, the induced voltage always acts to oppose the change that causes it; this is why the primary draws only a small magnetising current when the secondary is open, and draws more current as the secondary load increases. This mutual action is called mutual induction.

    Vs / Vp = Ns / Np Is / Ip = Np / Ns (ideal) Vp × Ip = Vs × Is (power in = power out) where Vp, Vs are primary and secondary RMS voltages, Np, Ns the numbers of turns and Ip, Is the currents.

    Worked example. A supply needs 12 V RMS from 230 V mains. The turns ratio Np : Ns = 230 : 12 = 19.2 : 1, so a primary of 1150 turns would need a secondary of 60 turns. If the secondary delivers 1 A, the ideal primary current is 12 × 1 / 230 = 0.052 A (52 mA). The secondary peak voltage is 12 × √2 = 17.0 V.

    Important facts about transformers:

    • A transformer works only on AC (a changing current). On DC the flux is constant, nothing is induced, and the primary would simply overheat, since only its small wire resistance limits the current.
    • The core is made from thin insulated steel sheets (laminations, often in E and I shapes) rather than a solid block, to reduce eddy currents, which are circulating currents induced in the core itself that waste power as heat. Ferrite cores are used at high frequencies (in SMPS).
    • Losses: copper loss (heating of the winding resistance, I²R), core loss (eddy current and hysteresis loss). Small mains transformers are typically 80 to 95% efficient, larger ones more. The small hum you hear from a transformer is caused by the core laminations vibrating at 100 Hz (magnetostriction).
    • A mains transformer for 50 Hz is heavy because at low frequency it needs a large core to carry the flux without saturating. This is the main reason linear power supplies are bulky.

    The diode: a one-way valve for current

    A semiconductor diode is a two-terminal device made by joining P-type and N-type silicon (a PN junction). Its terminals are the anode (P side) and the cathode (N side, marked with a band on the body). When the anode is more positive than the cathode by about 0.7 V (for silicon) the diode is forward biased and conducts with little resistance. When the voltage is reversed the diode is reverse biased and only a tiny leakage current flows. A water analogy is a non-return valve in a pipe.

    Two ratings matter in rectifiers:

    • Maximum forward current, IF (for example 1 A for the common 1N4007).
    • Peak inverse voltage (PIV), also called peak reverse voltage: the largest reverse voltage the diode can withstand without breaking down (1000 V for the 1N4007). The PIV a diode meets in a circuit must be below its rating, with a safety margin.

    Rectifier diodes in power supplies are ordinary silicon diodes. Schottky diodes (forward drop about 0.3 to 0.5 V) are used in low-voltage and switching supplies to reduce loss. Historical rectifiers included vacuum-tube diodes, mercury-arc valves and copper-oxide and selenium plates; today silicon devices are used almost everywhere.

    Half-wave rectifier

    Figure 1.5b. Half-wave rectifier. The diode conducts during the positive half cycles and blocks the negative half cycles.

    Working. During the positive half cycle of the secondary voltage, the top end of the secondary is positive, the diode is forward biased and current flows through the load RL. The output follows the input (less about 0.7 V). During the negative half cycle the diode is reverse biased, no current flows and the output is zero. The output is therefore a series of positive humps with gaps between them, repeating at 50 Hz.

    Vdc = Vm / π = 0.318 Vm Irms = Im / 2 ripple factor = 1.21 ripple frequency = f = 50 Hz max efficiency = 40.6% PIV = Vm TUF = 0.287 where Vm is the peak secondary voltage (diode drop ignored), Im the peak load current and TUF the transformer utilisation factor (DC output power divided by the transformer's VA rating).

    Example: with 12 V RMS on the secondary, Vm = 17.0 V and the average DC output of a half-wave rectifier (ignoring the diode drop) is 17.0 / π = 5.40 V.

    Drawbacks: only half the input cycle is used, the DC output is low, the ripple is very large (the ripple RMS is larger than the DC value, ripple factor 1.21) and the ripple is at 50 Hz, which needs a bigger filter capacitor. The DC current in the secondary also tends to magnetise (saturate) the transformer core. Half-wave rectifiers are used only for very small currents, for example in signal detectors and some battery chargers.

    Full-wave centre-tap rectifier

    Figure 1.5c. Full-wave rectifier using a centre-tapped transformer and two diodes. The centre tap is the 0 V reference.

    Working. The centre tap divides the secondary into two halves whose voltages VA and VB are equal and opposite. In the positive half cycle the top end is positive: D1 conducts and current flows through RL from the + terminal back to the centre tap, while D2 is reverse biased. In the negative half cycle the bottom end is positive: D2 conducts and current flows through RL in the same direction as before, while D1 is off. The load therefore receives a hump in every half cycle.

    Vdc = 2Vm / π = 0.637 Vm Irms = Im / √2 ripple factor = 0.482 ripple frequency = 2f = 100 Hz max efficiency = 81.2% PIV = 2Vm TUF = 0.693 where Vm is the peak voltage of one half of the secondary.

    Why is the PIV 2Vm? When D1 is off, its anode is at −Vm (bottom of the top half) and its cathode is held at +Vm by the conducting D2 and the output, so the reverse voltage across it is 2Vm. A 12-0-12 V transformer (Vm = 17.0 V per half) therefore needs diodes rated above 34 V PIV.

    Advantages over half wave: double the average DC output, much lower ripple, ripple at 100 Hz (easier to filter), no DC saturation of the core. Disadvantages: needs a centre-tapped transformer, each half of the secondary works only half the time, and the diodes must withstand 2Vm.

    Full-wave bridge rectifier

    Figure 1.5d. Bridge rectifier. D1 and D2 conduct on one half cycle, D3 and D4 on the other; the load current always flows in the same direction.

    Working. Four diodes are connected in a diamond (bridge). The AC is applied to two opposite corners and the DC is taken from the other two. In the positive half cycle (top of secondary positive) current flows through D1, down through RL from + to −, and back through D2 to the bottom of the secondary. In the negative half cycle current flows through D3, through RL in the same direction, and back through D4. Two diodes are in the current path at any instant, so the output peak is about Vm − 1.4 V.

    Vdc = 2Vm / π ripple frequency = 100 Hz ripple factor = 0.482 max efficiency = 81.2% PIV = Vm per diode TUF = 0.812 where Vm is the peak voltage of the whole secondary.

    Advantages: no centre tap needed, whole secondary used all the time (best transformer utilisation), PIV only Vm. Disadvantages: four diodes and two diode drops, which matter in low-voltage supplies. Bridge rectifiers are sold as a single four-pin package with the terminals marked ~, ~, + and −, and are by far the most common rectifier in mains equipment and inside every SMPS.

    Filters: turning pulses into smooth DC

    The rectifier output contains a DC component plus large AC components at the ripple frequency (50 or 100 Hz) and its harmonics. A filter passes the DC to the load and removes the AC. It uses two facts:

    • A capacitor has reactance XC = 1 / (2πfC). It blocks DC (infinite reactance at f = 0) and offers a low-impedance path to AC. Placed in parallel (shunt) with the load, it bypasses the ripple.
    • An inductor (choke) has reactance XL = 2πfL. It passes DC with only its small winding resistance and opposes AC. Placed in series with the load, it blocks the ripple.

    Figure 1.5e. The three classic power-supply filters placed between the rectifier and the load.

    Capacitor (shunt) filter

    A large electrolytic capacitor (hundreds to thousands of microfarads) is connected across the load. As the rectified voltage rises towards its peak, the diode conducts and the capacitor charges to nearly Vm. After the peak the input falls faster than the capacitor can discharge, so the diode switches off and the capacitor alone supplies the load, its voltage falling slowly. At the next peak the diode conducts briefly and recharges it. The output is a DC level with a small sawtooth ripple.

    Vr(p-p) ≈ IL / (fr × C) Vdc ≈ Vpeak - Vr(p-p) / 2 where IL is the load current (A), fr the ripple frequency (50 Hz half wave, 100 Hz full wave), C the capacitance (F) and Vpeak the peak of the rectified voltage.

    Worked example. A bridge rectifier is fed from a 12 V RMS secondary and supplies 200 mA through a 2200 µF capacitor. Peak rectified voltage = 17.0 − 1.4 = 15.6 V. Ripple = 0.2 / (100 × 0.0022) = 0.91 V peak to peak. DC output ≈ 15.6 − 0.45 = 15.1 V. With a half-wave rectifier and the same capacitor the ripple frequency is 50 Hz and the ripple doubles to about 1.8 V.

    Points to remember: ripple rises with load current and falls with larger C or a full-wave rectifier; the diode conducts only in short, high current pulses near each peak; at switch-on the empty capacitor draws a large inrush current, which is why a fuse must be of the slow-blow (time-delay) type. The capacitor filter is simple and cheap and gives a high output voltage, so it is used in almost every small supply, but its regulation is poor at high load currents.

    LC (choke-input) filter

    An inductor L in series is followed by a capacitor C across the load. The inductor opposes any change in current: when the current tries to rise it stores energy in its magnetic field, and when it tries to fall it releases that energy, so the current through it stays nearly constant. The capacitor then bypasses whatever ripple is left. Because the ripple is reduced twice, once by the high XL and once by the low XC, the LC filter gives low ripple and, unlike the capacitor filter, its ripple does not increase much with load current. Its output voltage is lower (close to the average 0.637 Vm) and the choke is heavy and bulky at 100 Hz. It is used for high-current supplies.

    Pi (CLC) filter

    A capacitor C1, then a series inductor L, then a second capacitor C2, drawn like the Greek letter π. It is also called a capacitor-input filter because the rectified voltage meets the capacitor first. C1 does most of the smoothing and gives a high output voltage (close to Vm); L blocks the ripple that remains on C1; C2 bypasses any ripple that gets through L. The result is very low ripple, useful for low-noise audio stages, at the cost of poorer regulation and more parts. In small circuits a resistor often replaces the inductor (an RC pi filter), for example to give a cleaner supply to a microphone preamplifier.

    Voltage regulators

    After filtering, the DC still carries ripple and it changes whenever the mains voltage or the load current changes. A voltage regulator keeps the output voltage constant regardless of these changes. Regulators are described by:

    Load regulation (%) = (VNL - VFL) / VFL × 100 Line regulation = change in Vout / change in Vin where VNL is the output at no load and VFL the output at full load. Smaller values mean better regulation.

    A regulator that mainly holds the output steady against input changes is sometimes called a line regulator, and one that mainly handles load changes a load regulator; in practice every good regulator does both.

    Zener diode shunt regulator

    A Zener diode is a diode designed to operate in reverse breakdown. Once the reverse voltage reaches its Zener voltage VZ (available from about 2.4 V to 200 V), the diode conducts and the voltage across it stays almost constant over a wide range of current. It is connected in reverse bias across the load, with a series resistor RS from the unregulated supply.

    Figure 1.5f. (a) Zener shunt regulator. (b) Three-terminal 78xx regulator with the input and output capacitors recommended in typical data sheets.

    Working. The series current IS = (Vin − VZ) / RS divides between the Zener (IZ) and the load (IL). If Vin rises, IS rises, but the extra current flows through the Zener while the output stays at VZ; the extra voltage is dropped across RS. If the load draws more current, the Zener current falls by the same amount and the output again stays at VZ. Regulation fails if IZ falls below a minimum (the knee current, typically a few milliamperes) or if the Zener's power rating P = VZ × IZ is exceeded.

    Rs = (Vin(min) - Vz) / (IL(max) + Iz(min)) Pz = Vz × Iz(max) where Vin(min) is the lowest input voltage, IL(max) the largest load current and Iz(min) the minimum Zener current for regulation.

    Worked example. A 5.1 V Zener regulator is fed from 12 V and must supply up to 20 mA, with at least 5 mA through the Zener. RS = (12 − 5.1) / 0.025 = 276 Ω; choose the standard value 270 Ω. Then IS = 6.9 / 270 = 25.6 mA. If the load is disconnected, all 25.6 mA flows in the Zener, dissipating 5.1 × 0.0256 = 0.13 W, so a 0.5 W Zener is comfortable. Zener regulators are simple but waste power and suit only small currents, such as reference voltages inside instruments.

    Series (linear) regulators and the 78xx family

    For larger currents a transistor is placed in series with the load and controlled by a feedback amplifier that compares the output with a Zener or band-gap reference. The transistor behaves like an automatically adjusted resistor: it drops whatever voltage is needed so the output stays correct. This is a linear regulator, because the transistor works in its linear (active) region.

    Three-terminal regulator ICs contain the reference, amplifier, pass transistor and protection (current limiting and thermal shutdown) in one package with pins IN, GND and OUT. The 78xx series gives fixed positive outputs (7805 gives +5 V, 7812 gives +12 V, 7815 gives +15 V, rated about 1 A with a heat sink); the 79xx series gives negative outputs for split supplies; the LM317 is adjustable, with Vout = 1.25 × (1 + R2 / R1) volts. These regulators need the input to be at least about 2 to 3 V above the output (the dropout voltage). Low-dropout (LDO) regulators work with only a few hundred millivolts of headroom and are used in battery devices. A regulator also reduces ripple strongly (ripple rejection is typically 60 to 80 dB).

    P(regulator) = (Vin - Vout) × IL efficiency ≈ Vout / Vin where the small current drawn by the regulator itself is ignored.

    Worked example. A 7805 supplies 0.5 A from a 9 V input. It dissipates (9 − 5) × 0.5 = 2 W as heat, so it needs a heat sink, and the efficiency is only about 5 / 9 = 56%. This waste is the main weakness of linear regulation.

    Linear versus switched-mode power supplies

    Figure 1.5g. (a) A linear power supply regulates by dropping excess voltage in a transistor. (b) A switched-mode supply rectifies the mains directly, chops it at high frequency and regulates by varying the duty cycle.

    Linear power supply (LPS)

    The linear supply is exactly the chain described so far: 50 Hz transformer, rectifier, capacitor filter and a linear regulator. The pass transistor operates continuously and the excess voltage is turned into heat. Linear supplies respond very quickly to load changes and are electrically quiet because nothing switches.

    Switched-mode power supply (SMPS)

    An SMPS uses a transistor as a fast switch (fully on or fully off) instead of as a variable resistor. A switch that is fully on has almost no voltage across it, and a switch that is fully off carries no current, so in both states it wastes very little power. Stages:

    1. Input rectifier and filter: the mains is rectified directly by a bridge without a 50 Hz transformer and smoothed by a high-voltage capacitor, giving about 325 V DC. An input EMI filter stops switching noise from leaking back into the mains.
    2. High-frequency switch: a power MOSFET chops this DC on and off at tens to hundreds of kilohertz, producing a high-frequency square wave.
    3. High-frequency transformer: a small ferrite-core transformer steps the voltage down and provides isolation. At 100 kHz the transformer can be thousands of times smaller than a 50 Hz one for the same power, because the core needed shrinks as frequency rises.
    4. Output rectifier and filter: fast (often Schottky) diodes and an LC filter convert the high-frequency pulses into smooth DC. Because the ripple frequency is high, small capacitors are enough.
    5. Control (feedback) unit: an error amplifier compares the output with a reference. The result is sent back across the isolation barrier, usually through an optocoupler (an LED and a light sensor in one package), to a pulse-width modulation (PWM) controller. Most controllers keep the switching frequency fixed and vary the duty cycle, the fraction of each period for which the switch is on. A larger duty cycle delivers more energy per cycle and raises the output.
    Buck converter: Vout = D × Vin (D = ton / T) where D is the duty cycle, ton the on time and T the switching period. For example 12 V to 5 V needs D ≈ 0.42.

    SMPS types by function: AC-DC converters (mains adapters, as above) and DC-DC converters (inside devices). By circuit: buck (steps down), boost (steps up), buck-boost, and the isolated flyback (low power, up to roughly 100 to 150 W, used in almost every phone charger and small medical adapter) and forward converters (medium power); other circuits such as Cuk and SEPIC are also used.

    Linear power supply

    • Advantages: simple circuit, very low output noise and ripple, fast transient response, no radio-frequency interference, reliable.
    • Disadvantages: heavy 50 Hz transformer and large capacitors, efficiency typically 30 to 60%, lots of heat, one transformer per output voltage, bulky.
    • Uses: low-noise preamplifiers, laboratory bench supplies, older audiometers and amplifiers, sensitive measurement circuits.

    Switched-mode power supply

    • Advantages: efficiency typically 80 to 95%, small and light, little heat, works over a wide input range (often 100 to 240 V), several outputs from one transformer.
    • Disadvantages: more complex circuit, output carries switching ripple and noise, can radiate electromagnetic interference (EMI), draws a peaky, harmonic-rich current from the mains unless it has power factor correction.
    • Uses: computer power supplies, phone, laptop and hearing-aid chargers, modern audiometers, UPS and inverters.

    Batteries in portable devices

    A battery stores energy chemically and delivers DC directly, so it needs no rectifier or filter; only a regulator or DC-DC converter is usually added. Hearing aids, cochlear implant processors, portable audiometers, screening OAE units and sound level meters all run from batteries.

    • Primary (single-use) cells cannot be recharged: zinc-air hearing-aid cells, alkaline AA/AAA cells, lithium coin cells.
    • Secondary (rechargeable) cells: lithium-ion (Li-ion), nickel-metal hydride (NiMH), lead-acid (in UPS, topic 1.7).
    • Capacity in milliampere-hours (mAh) or ampere-hours (Ah): the charge the battery can deliver. Energy in watt-hours (Wh) = voltage × Ah. C-rate: charge or discharge current relative to capacity (1C for a 2000 mAh cell is 2 A).
    • Internal resistance: causes the terminal voltage to drop when current is drawn; it rises as a battery ages or runs down. Self-discharge: slow loss of charge in storage. Cycle life: number of charge-discharge cycles before capacity falls to a set fraction (often 80%).

    • Diagnostic audiometers, immittance meters and OAE or ABR systems contain internal power supplies (now mostly SMPS with linear post-regulation). Noise on these supplies can raise the noise floor of test signals and recordings.

    Key points to remember

    • Regulated DC supply: transformer, rectifier, filter, regulator, load.
    • Transformer: Vs / Vp = Ns / Np; works only on AC; laminated core reduces eddy currents.
    • Half wave: Vdc = 0.318 Vm, ripple 50 Hz, ripple factor 1.21, PIV = Vm.
    • Centre tap: Vdc = 0.637 Vm, ripple 100 Hz, PIV = 2Vm. Bridge: Vdc = 0.637 Vm, 100 Hz, PIV = Vm, two diode drops.
    • Capacitor filter ripple: Vr(p-p) ≈ IL / (fr C); LC and pi filters give lower ripple.
    • Zener: shunt regulator; Rs = (Vin(min) − Vz) / (IL(max) + Iz(min)).
    • 78xx: fixed positive regulators, need about 2 to 3 V headroom; power wasted = (Vin − Vout) × IL.
    • Linear: quiet but inefficient and heavy. SMPS: efficient and light, but noisier; regulated by PWM duty cycle.
    • Battery life ≈ capacity (mAh) / current (mA); zinc-air hearing-aid cells are about 1.4 V.

    AC power supply, earthing and isolation transformers

    B.Sc. B2.42 hoursUnit 1

    The mains socket in a clinic delivers 230 V AC at 50 Hz through three wires: live, neutral and earth. Knowing what these wires do, why equipment must be earthed, how leakage current can reach a patient and how isolation transformers, stabilisers and surge protectors help, is basic safety knowledge for anyone who connects electrodes, earphones or bone vibrators to a person.

    What it is

    The AC supply is the sinusoidal voltage distributed by the electricity board. Earthing is the deliberate connection of the supply neutral and of equipment metal parts to the general mass of the earth. An isolation transformer is a 1:1 transformer that supplies equipment without any conductive connection to the mains.

    Why it is required

    Mains voltage can kill. Earthing makes faults trip the protection instead of making a case live; isolation limits the current that can flow through a patient; stabilisers and surge protectors keep sensitive audiology equipment working correctly and prevent damage.

    How it works

    Current always needs a closed path back to its source. Earthing gives fault current a low-resistance path so the fuse, MCB or RCCB disconnects the supply. An isolation transformer removes the path through earth, so touching one wire drives almost no current through the body.

    AC basics: amplitude, frequency, RMS and peak

    An alternating voltage changes direction periodically. The mains follows a sine wave:

    v(t) = Vp sin(2πft) T = 1 / f where v(t) is the instantaneous voltage, Vp the peak (maximum) value, f the frequency in hertz (Hz) and T the period in seconds.

    Figure 1.6a. The Indian mains voltage: 230 V RMS, 325 V peak, 650 V peak to peak, period 20 ms.

    • Frequency f: number of complete cycles per second. India uses 50 Hz (the Americas largely use 60 Hz), so one cycle lasts T = 1 / 50 = 20 ms.
    • Peak value Vp: the largest value in either direction. Peak-to-peak Vpp = 2Vp.
    • Average value: over a full cycle a sine averages to zero; over one half cycle it averages 0.637 Vp (this is the rectifier DC value from topic 1.5).
    • RMS value (root mean square): the DC voltage that would produce the same heating in a resistor. It is found by squaring the waveform, taking the mean over a cycle and then the square root. For a sine wave VRMS = Vp / √2 = 0.707 Vp.
    Vrms = Vp / √2 Vp = √2 × Vrms form factor = Vrms / Vavg = 1.11 (sine) crest factor = Vp / Vrms = 1.414 (sine) where Vavg is the half-cycle average. Other waveforms have different factors: a square wave has Vrms = Vp, a triangle wave Vrms = Vp / √3.

    Worked example. The declared mains voltage is 230 V RMS. Peak = 230 × 1.414 = 325 V; peak to peak = 650 V. A 230 V lamp on DC and on AC gives the same brightness because the RMS value is defined by heating. Whenever a voltage or current is quoted for mains without further qualification, it is an RMS value, and ordinary multimeters display RMS (many cheaper meters are only accurate for pure sine waves; "true RMS" meters handle distorted waveforms).

    Clinical linkThe same RMS idea is used for sound. Sound pressure level in dB SPL is based on the RMS sound pressure, and audiometer calibration voltages are measured as RMS values. A pure tone with an RMS pressure of 1 Pa has a peak pressure of 1.41 Pa (94 dB SPL).

    Why the grid uses AC: AC voltage can be stepped up and down efficiently with transformers. Power is generated at around 11 to 25 kV, stepped up to hundreds of kilovolts for long-distance transmission (a higher voltage means lower current and lower I²R loss in the lines), stepped down to 11 kV for local distribution, and finally to 400/230 V by the distribution transformer near the building.

    Single-phase and three-phase supply

    A single-phase supply uses two current-carrying wires: the phase (live, L) and the neutral (N), plus the protective earth. The voltage between L and N in India is 230 V. Because the voltage passes through zero twice every cycle, the power delivered to a resistive load is not constant: it pulses at 100 Hz, from zero up to twice the average value. The voltage peaks occur at 90° and 270° of each 360° cycle.

    A three-phase supply has three live conductors, traditionally called R, Y and B (red, yellow, blue) in India (L1, L2, L3 internationally), carrying sine waves displaced by 120° (one third of a cycle, 6.67 ms), usually with a neutral in a star (wye) connection. In each cycle each phase reaches its positive peak once and its negative peak once, so there is always at least one phase near its peak and the total power to a balanced load is constant.

    V(line) = √3 × V(phase) = 1.732 × 230 ≈ 400 V where V(phase) is measured from any phase to neutral and V(line) between any two phases (star connection).

    Live, neutral and earth

    • Live (L, phase): carries the supply voltage, 230 V relative to earth. Fuses, MCBs and switches must be placed in the live conductor so that switching off makes the equipment safe.
    • Neutral (N): the return conductor, connected to earth at the distribution transformer. It is normally close to earth potential, typically within a few volts, but it carries the full load current and must be treated as live.
    • Earth (E, protective earth, PE): a safety conductor that normally carries no current. It connects the exposed metal parts of equipment to the earth electrode.

    Colour codes: in older Indian wiring live is red, neutral black and earth green; newer installations follow the international (IEC) colours: brown live, blue neutral and green-with-yellow-stripe earth. Indian three-pin plugs and sockets have the earth pin at the top, thicker and longer than the others, so that it connects first and disconnects last.

    Earthing: why and how

    Earthing (called grounding in American usage) means connecting parts of the electrical system to the earth through a buried electrode. Two kinds are needed:

    • System earthing: the neutral of the distribution transformer (or generator) is connected to earth at the source. This fixes the voltage of the whole system relative to earth, limits overvoltages from lightning or faults and gives fault current a path back to the source.
    • Equipment (protective) earthing: every exposed metal part of Class I equipment (cases, chassis, trolleys) is connected by the green or green-yellow wire to the earth pin and on to the earth electrode.

    Why earthing is needed. Suppose the insulation inside an audiometer fails and the live wire touches the metal case. Without earthing, the case sits at 230 V and anyone who touches it while standing on the floor completes a circuit through their body to earth. With a good earth the fault current flows through the earth wire, which has a resistance of a fraction of an ohm, so the current is large (tens to hundreds of amperes) and the fuse or MCB opens within a fraction of a second. The case is held close to earth potential until then. A residual current device (RCCB or RCD) adds a second layer: it compares the current in live and neutral and trips, typically in less than 40 ms, if more than its rated difference (30 mA for personnel protection) is leaking to earth.

    Other benefits: a stable 0 V reference for electronics, a path to drain static charge and surge current, and screening of cables and cases against interference (hum).

    Equipotential bonding and ground loops

    When several devices surround a patient (for example an ABR amplifier, a PC and a printer), all their earths should be connected to one common point so that no voltage difference exists between metal parts a patient or clinician might touch at the same time. This is called equipotential bonding. If devices are earthed at different sockets, small voltage differences between those earths drive current through the signal cable screens, forming a ground loop that injects 50 Hz hum into the audio or EEG signal. The cure is a single-point (star) earth: power all interconnected equipment from one outlet strip or isolation transformer.

    Electric shock, leakage current and patient safety

    The danger of electricity is the current through the body, and it depends on the voltage and the body's resistance. Dry skin may have a resistance of tens of kilohms, wet skin or skin prepared with electrode gel much less, down to around a kilohm. Approximate effects of 50 Hz current flowing hand to hand or hand to foot for about a second are summarised below; the thresholds vary widely between people.

    Leakage current

    Leakage current is the small current that flows from the mains parts of equipment to its case, to earth or to the patient, even when nothing is faulty. It arises because insulation is never perfect and, mainly, because of stray capacitance between the live wiring and nearby metal, plus the deliberate capacitors of the mains interference filter. A capacitor passes AC current:

    I = 2π f C V where I is the leakage current (A), f = 50 Hz, C the stray or filter capacitance (F) and V the mains voltage (230 V).

    Worked example. A stray capacitance of 2 nF from live to the case gives I = 2 × 3.1416 × 50 × 2 × 10−9 × 230 = 145 µA. Normally this current flows harmlessly to earth through the protective earth wire. If the earth wire is broken, it flows through anyone who touches the case: harmless to most people for a 145 µA current, but it shows why a broken earth removes a layer of safety and why the limits for equipment connected to patients are low.

    The medical equipment safety standard IEC 60601-1 classifies equipment and sets leakage limits:

    • Class I: basic insulation plus protective earth (three-pin plug). Class II: double or reinforced insulation, no earth needed (square-in-square symbol). Internally powered: battery equipment.
    • Applied part: the part that touches the patient (earphones, bone vibrator, probe, electrodes). Types: B (body), BF (body, floating: the applied part is isolated from earth) and CF (cardiac, floating: suitable for direct cardiac contact). Audiometer transducers and evoked-potential electrodes are typically type B or BF.
    • Typical patient leakage limits are of the order of 100 µA in normal condition for B and BF applied parts and 10 µA for CF parts, with higher values allowed under a single fault. Check the current edition of the standard for exact values.
    SafetyIn electrophysiology (ABR, ECochG, VEMP, ASSR) the skin is abraded and gel is applied to lower electrode impedance, so the patient's skin resistance is low exactly where a conductor is attached. Infants and young children are tested this way. Never connect a patient to equipment with a damaged cable, a missing earth pin, a two-pin adapter on a three-pin plug or an extension board lying on a wet floor.

    A medical electrical system is a combination of medical and non-medical equipment, such as an ABR amplifier connected by USB to an ordinary office PC. Within the patient environment (the space within which the patient can touch equipment, often taken as about 1.5 m around the patient), the combination must still meet the medical leakage limits. The usual solutions are a medical-grade PC and power supply, or powering the whole cart (PC, monitor, printer, amplifier) through a medical isolation transformer.

    Isolation transformers

    An isolation transformer has a 1:1 turns ratio: 230 V in, 230 V out. It exists for isolation, not voltage change. Primary and secondary windings are magnetically coupled but electrically separate, insulated from each other for several kilovolts, and a medical isolation transformer usually has an earthed electrostatic screen (a copper foil) between the windings.

    Figure 1.6d. A medical isolation transformer supplying an ABR system. The secondary output floats (neither wire is connected to earth); the protective earth still connects every metal case.

    Principle. The secondary voltage is induced magnetically, so the secondary circuit has no conductive connection to the mains neutral or to earth. If a person standing on the floor touches one secondary wire, there is no return path for current except the tiny stray capacitance between the secondary circuit and earth, typically well under a few nanofarads, so the current is in the microampere range instead of the tens or hundreds of milliamperes from an earth-referenced live wire. The earthed screen diverts capacitively coupled noise and leakage from the primary to earth instead of into the secondary.

    Uses in speech and hearing practice:

    • Powering evoked-potential (ABR, ASSR, ECochG) and OAE systems together with their PC and monitor, so that the combined leakage current stays within medical limits.
    • Powering computer-based audiometers and speech-analysis workstations where a non-medical PC is within reach of the patient.
    • Reducing common-mode electrical noise and hum reaching sensitive recording equipment.
    • Workshop use for safe servicing of mains equipment (by trained staff).

    Limitations. An isolation transformer does not protect against touching both secondary wires at once (the full 230 V then drives current through the body); it does not remove the need for protective earthing of metal cases; it must be rated for the total load (typically 500 VA to 2 kVA for a clinical cart) and it does not correct voltage fluctuations. A mains transformer inside a device (topic 1.5) also provides isolation for the low-voltage circuits, which is why battery-powered or transformer-isolated devices are inherently safer.

    DefinitionAn autotransformer (variac) has a single winding tapped at different points. It changes voltage but gives no isolation, so it must never be used where an isolation transformer is required.

    50 Hz hum in audiology equipment

    Hum is interference at the mains frequency (50 Hz) and its harmonics (100, 150 Hz and so on) that appears in audio signals and physiological recordings. It gets in by three routes:

    1. Electric field (capacitive) coupling from mains cables to high-impedance inputs such as unscreened electrode leads.
    2. Magnetic field (inductive) coupling from transformers, motors and cables carrying large currents into loops of wire (electrode leads, microphone cables).
    3. Conducted interference through ground loops and noisy power supplies (ripple from a failing filter capacitor appears at 100 Hz).

    Remedies: keep electrode leads short and twisted together, keep them away from power cables and transformers; keep electrode impedances low and balanced so the differential amplifier can reject common-mode hum; use screened cables with the screen earthed at one end; power interconnected equipment from one point; use an isolation transformer; use battery power where possible. ABR systems also offer a 50 Hz notch filter, which should be used with care because it can distort the response. A sound-treated test booth usually has its own earthed screening and filtered power, discussed in the acoustics units.

    Voltage fluctuations, stabilisers and surge protection

    The mains voltage is not perfectly steady. Common disturbances include under-voltage (brown-out) during peak demand, over-voltage (for example when a neutral connection is lost), sags and swells lasting a few cycles, spikes or transients of hundreds to thousands of volts lasting microseconds (lightning, switching of motors and air conditioners) and complete outages. In parts of India supply voltages can drift well outside 230 V ± 10%, especially in rural areas.

    Voltage stabilisers

    A voltage stabiliser is an AC device that keeps its output near 230 V (or 400 V three phase) while the input varies over a wide range. It works by boost (adding voltage when the input is low) and buck (subtracting voltage when the input is high) using transformer windings.

    • Relay (step) type: an electronic circuit compares the output with a reference and uses relays to select different taps on an autotransformer. It is cheap and light, corrects in steps (output typically within about ±5 to 10%) and is used for refrigerators, air conditioners and televisions.
    • Servo-controlled type: a servo motor moves a carbon brush along a variable autotransformer (variac), which feeds the primary of a buck-boost transformer whose secondary is in series with the supply. The control card continuously compares the output with a reference and drives the motor to correct it. Servo stabilisers give smooth, accurate output (typically ±1 to 2%) over wide input ranges and handle large loads, so they are used for hospital equipment, audiology rooms and laboratories. They are slower (correction takes a fraction of a second to seconds) and need periodic maintenance of the brush and motor.
    • Static (solid-state) type: uses power electronic switches (thyristors or IGBTs) instead of a motor; fast and maintenance free, but more expensive.

    Figure 1.6e. Servo-controlled voltage stabiliser. The control card senses the output and drives the servo motor, which sets the variac so the buck-boost transformer adds or subtracts the right correction voltage.

    Surge protection

    Stabilisers are too slow to stop microsecond spikes. Surge protective devices (SPDs), built mainly from metal oxide varistors (MOVs) and gas discharge tubes, conduct heavily when the voltage exceeds a threshold and divert the surge energy to earth. Buildings use coordinated stages: at the main panel, at distribution boards and at the point of use (a "spike buster" extension board contains MOVs). SPDs work only with a good earth and wear out after large surges, so indicator lamps should be checked.

    Current developments

    • Smart energy meters and power-quality monitors let hospitals log voltage sags, swells and outages and correlate them with equipment faults.
    • Hospitals increasingly adopt IT (isolated) power systems with insulation monitoring in critical areas, extending the isolation-transformer principle to whole rooms.

    Key points to remember

    • Indian mains: 230 V RMS, 325 V peak, 50 Hz, period 20 ms; three phase 400 V line to line.
    • Vrms = Vp / √2 for a sine; RMS is the heating-equivalent value.
    • Live carries the voltage, neutral is the return (earthed at source), earth is a safety conductor that normally carries no current.
    • Earthing lets fault current trip the fuse, MCB or RCCB and holds metal cases near earth potential.
    • Leakage current I = 2πfCV flows through stray capacitance; limits for patient-connected parts are of the order of 100 µA.
    • An isolation transformer (1:1) removes the path to earth, so touching one output wire drives only a tiny current.
    • Hum (50, 100, 150 Hz) enters by capacitive, magnetic and ground-loop paths; twisted short leads and single-point earthing reduce it.
    • Servo stabilisers correct slow voltage changes accurately; surge protectors handle fast spikes; a UPS handles outages.

    UPS and inverters

    B.Sc. B2.41.5 hoursUnit 1

    A power cut in the middle of an ABR recording, a hearing-aid programming session or a speech recording can lose data, corrupt a device or force the patient to return. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) or an inverter keeps equipment running from batteries when the mains fails. Choosing the right type and size, and understanding VA, watts and backup time, lets a clinic protect both its patients' time and its equipment.

    What it is

    A UPS is a unit containing a battery, a charger and an inverter that supplies AC power to equipment without interruption (or with a break of only a few milliseconds) when the mains fails. An inverter is the circuit that converts DC from a battery into AC; "home inverters" are larger battery backup systems for whole rooms.

    Why it is required

    Mains supply in many places has outages, sags and spikes. Computers and computer-based audiometers reset when power is lost even for a fraction of a second, and an interrupted hearing-aid firmware update or programming session can leave the aid unusable until reprogrammed.

    How it works

    While the mains is present, the charger keeps the battery full. When the mains fails, the inverter switches transistors on and off to turn battery DC into 230 V, 50 Hz AC. Depending on the design, the inverter either takes over through a fast transfer switch or is running all the time.

    Why clinics need backup power

    • Data loss: an unsaved audiogram, ABR averaging run or voice recording is lost if the PC shuts down.
    • Device programming: fitting software writes settings (and sometimes firmware) to hearing aids and cochlear implant processors; interruption can leave the device in an incomplete state.
    • Patient time: infants tested during natural sleep for ABR may not be testable again that day.
    • Equipment protection: repeated abrupt power loss can damage hard disks and corrupt calibration or configuration files; online UPS units also filter spikes and voltage fluctuations.
    • Continuity of tele-services: routers and network switches on a UPS keep tele-audiology sessions running through short outages (see M.Sc. 2.5 Tele-diagnosis).

    Building blocks and key terms

    Every UPS contains four blocks: a rectifier-charger (AC to DC, keeps the battery charged), a battery (usually sealed lead-acid, increasingly lithium), an inverter (DC to AC) and some form of switching or bypass. The three UPS types differ only in how these are arranged.

    • Transfer time: the gap in output when the UPS switches from mains to battery. Computer power supplies can usually ride through about 10 ms or more because of their internal capacitors.
    • Backup time (runtime): how long the battery can supply a given load.
    • VA rating (apparent power) and watt rating (real power): both limits must be respected, explained below.
    • Output waveform: square, modified (stepped) sine or pure sine.

    Types of UPS

    Figure 1.7a. The three UPS topologies. Arrows show the direction of power flow.

    (a) Offline (standby) UPS

    In normal operation the mains passes through a surge filter and the transfer switch directly to the load, while a small charger keeps the battery topped up. The inverter is off. When the mains fails or goes outside limits, a sensing circuit starts the inverter and the transfer switch (a relay) changes over, typically within about 2 to 10 ms. Advantages: simple, cheap, efficient (the load runs directly from mains most of the time). Disadvantages: the load sees mains disturbances such as sags and small swells; there is a short break at changeover; output on battery is often a stepped wave. Typical ratings: up to about 1 to 2 kVA. Use: single desktop PCs and small devices.

    (b) Line-interactive UPS

    This is the standby design with two improvements. An automatic voltage regulator (AVR), a transformer with buck and boost taps switched by relays, corrects moderate under- and over-voltage without using the battery. The inverter and charger are one bidirectional converter that stays connected to the output: it charges the battery when the mains is present and supplies the load when it fails, so changeover is faster and smoother (typically a few milliseconds). Advantages: handles common brown-outs without draining the battery, good efficiency, moderate cost. Disadvantages: still a short transfer time, and the load is not fully isolated from mains noise. Typical ratings: about 0.5 to 5 kVA. Use: clinic workstations, audiometer and PC sets, small servers.

    (c) Online double-conversion UPS

    All the power is converted twice, all the time: the rectifier-charger turns the incoming AC into DC on an internal DC bus, which also holds the battery, and the inverter continuously turns that DC back into a freshly generated, clean 230 V, 50 Hz sine wave. When the mains fails, the rectifier simply stops and the battery keeps the DC bus up, so there is no transfer and no break at all. A static bypass switch connects the load directly to the mains only if the inverter fails or is overloaded. Advantages: zero transfer time, output fully isolated from mains sags, swells, frequency variation and noise, pure sine output. Disadvantages: highest cost, some energy lost in double conversion (typical efficiency about 88 to 95%, with an "eco mode" available on many units), fan noise and heat. Typical ratings: 1 kVA upwards to hundreds of kVA. Use: evoked-potential systems, hospital servers, central clinic supply, any sensitive or critical equipment.

    The inverter: turning DC into AC

    An inverter converts DC into AC. It does not create energy: the energy comes from the battery (or a solar panel), and the inverter only changes its form. The basic circuit is the H-bridge: four electronic switches (MOSFETs or IGBTs) arranged around the load like the letter H.

    Figure 1.7b. H-bridge inverter. Closing S1 and S4 drives current from A to B; closing S2 and S3 drives it from B to A. Alternating the pairs 50 times per second gives a 50 Hz square wave.

    Working. When S1 and S4 are on, point A connects to battery + and point B to battery −, so the load sees +V. When S2 and S3 are on, the polarity reverses and the load sees −V. Alternating these pairs every 10 ms gives a 50 Hz square wave. A control circuit makes sure the two switches in the same leg are never on together, which would short the battery. Because a battery gives only 12, 24 or 48 V, the output is stepped up to 230 V either by a 50 Hz transformer after the bridge (common in home inverters) or by first boosting the DC to about 350 V with a high-frequency DC-DC converter and then using a high-voltage bridge (lighter, common in small UPS).

    Output waveforms

    Figure 1.7c. Inverter output waveforms. The stepped wave matches both the peak (325 V) and the RMS (230 V) of the mains; the square wave shown has the correct RMS but only 230 V peak.

    • Square wave: the simplest H-bridge output. Rich in odd harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th...), total harmonic distortion (THD) about 48%. Causes buzzing in transformers and motors and can overheat them. Rarely used now.
    • Modified sine (quasi-sine, stepped): the bridge also produces a zero-voltage step, giving +V, 0, −V, 0. By choosing the on time (half of each half cycle here) the wave has the correct peak and RMS, so equipment that rectifies to the peak, such as an SMPS, gets the right voltage. Its THD is still high (about 48% with this timing, about 31% with a wider two-thirds on time), so fans and transformers may hum and some equipment misbehaves. Common in low-cost standby UPS and inverters.
    • Pure sine wave: the switches are driven by high-frequency pulse-width modulation (PWM), where the width of rapid pulses follows a sine pattern, and an LC filter removes the switching frequency. THD is typically below about 3 to 5%, close to good mains. Recommended for medical and audio equipment, where harmonics could appear as hum or interfere with transformers and analog circuits.
    Clinical linkAn audiometer or amplifier with a 50 Hz linear transformer, or anything with an audio output, should run from a pure-sine inverter or online UPS. A stepped waveform can make transformers buzz audibly and can increase hum in test signals; this is unacceptable in a sound-treated room.

    Home inverters versus UPS

    What is sold in India as a "home inverter" is a large battery backup: an inverter-charger with one or more 100 to 200 Ah tubular lead-acid batteries, designed for hours of backup for lights, fans and computers. Home inverters usually have a longer changeover time (though many offer a faster "UPS mode", typically around 10 ms or less) and some have stepped outputs. A UPS has smaller batteries (minutes of backup) but faster or zero changeover and better output. Clinics often combine them: a home inverter or generator for long outages and a UPS at each critical workstation. Inverters are also classified as stand-alone (supply a separate load circuit from a battery) or grid-tie (feed solar power into the mains supply, synchronised with it).

    Sizing: VA, watts, power factor and backup time

    For AC loads there are two kinds of power:

    • Apparent power S = VRMS × IRMS, in volt-amperes (VA). It is what the wiring, switches and inverter transistors must carry.
    • Real power P, in watts (W). It is what is actually consumed and turned into heat, light, sound or computation, and what drains the battery.

    The ratio P / S is the power factor (pf). It is 1 for a heater or filament lamp, and less than 1 for loads that store energy in inductors or capacitors or that draw current in short peaks (motors, fluorescent tubes, SMPS without power factor correction). Modern computer supplies with power factor correction have pf close to 0.9 to 1.

    S (VA) = V × I P (W) = S × pf UPS rating ≥ total VA × 1.2 to 1.25 (margin) Energy (Wh) = battery V × Ah backup (h) ≈ (V × Ah × usable fraction × eta) / P where pf is the power factor, eta the inverter efficiency (typically 0.8 to 0.9) and usable fraction the share of capacity you allow to be used (depth of discharge).

    A UPS has both a VA rating and a watt rating, for example 1000 VA / 600 W for many small units (output pf 0.6) or 1000 VA / 900 W for many online units. The load must stay within both.

    Worked example 1: choosing a UPS for an audiometry workstation

    Loads: desktop PC 150 W, monitor 30 W, computer-based audiometer 40 W, immittance meter 30 W. Total P = 250 W. Assuming an overall pf of 0.8, S = 250 / 0.8 = 312.5 VA. With a 25% margin the requirement is about 390 VA and 250 W. A 600 VA / 360 W line-interactive UPS meets both limits; a 1 kVA unit gives room for growth and longer backup. Laser printers and room heaters should not be connected to a UPS, because their large surge currents can overload it.

    Worked example 2: backup time

    The chosen 1 kVA UPS has two 12 V, 7 Ah batteries in series (24 V, 7 Ah). Stored energy = 24 × 7 = 168 Wh. Allowing 80% usable capacity and 85% inverter efficiency, backup ≈ 168 × 0.8 × 0.85 / 250 = 0.46 h, about 27 minutes. In practice the result is shorter, because lead-acid batteries deliver less than their rated capacity at high currents (the Peukert effect): here the battery current is about 12 A, far above the 0.35 A at which a 7 Ah battery is rated over 20 hours. With a typical Peukert correction the backup falls to roughly 15 minutes. Manufacturers publish runtime charts, which are the best guide.

    Worked example 3: sizing a battery for a required time

    A clinic wants 2 hours of backup for a 250 W load from a 12 V inverter with 85% efficiency, using only 50% of the lead-acid battery capacity to extend its life. Energy from the battery = 250 × 2 / 0.85 = 588 Wh. Battery energy needed = 588 / 0.5 = 1176 Wh. Capacity at 12 V = 1176 / 12 = 98 Ah, so a 12 V, 100 Ah battery is the minimum; a larger one (for example 150 Ah) allows for ageing and the Peukert effect.

    RememberSealed lead-acid UPS batteries typically last about 3 to 5 years, less in hot rooms (high temperature roughly halves life for every 8 to 10 °C above about 25 °C). Deep discharges also shorten life. Test the UPS on battery periodically and replace batteries when backup time falls noticeably.

    Key points to remember

    • UPS = charger + battery + inverter + switching; it keeps equipment running through outages.
    • Offline: mains direct, inverter starts on failure, 2 to 10 ms transfer, cheapest.
    • Line-interactive: adds AVR (buck/boost) and bidirectional inverter-charger; handles brown-outs.
    • Online double conversion: AC to DC to AC all the time, zero transfer, cleanest output, static bypass.
    • Inverter: H-bridge switches DC alternately; outputs square, modified sine or PWM pure sine.

    • P (W) = S (VA) × pf; size the UPS on both VA and W with about 25% margin.

    Decimal and binary number systems

    B.Sc. B2.41.5 hoursUnit 1

    Every digital device in a speech and hearing clinic, from a hearing aid to a laptop running Praat, stores sounds, settings and test results as patterns of 0s and 1s. This topic explains how ordinary decimal numbers are written in binary, how to convert in both directions, and why the number of bits used for a sound decides how quiet and how loud a sound the device can handle.

    What it is

    A number system is a set of symbols (digits) and a rule for giving each digit a value by its position. Decimal uses ten digits (0 to 9) and powers of 10. Binary uses two digits (0 and 1) and powers of 2.

    Why it is required

    Electronic circuits can tell two states apart very reliably (a switch on or off, a voltage high or low). Binary matches these two states, so all digital audio, hearing-aid settings and patient files are stored and processed as binary numbers.

    How it works

    Each binary digit (bit) is worth twice the bit to its right. To convert decimal to binary, divide repeatedly by 2 and collect the remainders. To convert binary to decimal, add the place values of the bits that are 1.

    Background: place value and number bases

    When you write the decimal number 352 you are using a positional number system. The same digit means different things in different places: the 3 is worth three hundreds, the 5 is worth five tens and the 2 is worth two ones. Each position is worth ten times the position to its right. The number ten is called the base or radix of the system.

    352 = 3 × 10² + 5 × 10¹ + 2 × 10⁰ = 300 + 50 + 2 where 10² = 100, 10¹ = 10 and 10⁰ = 1 (any number raised to the power 0 is 1).

    Nothing is special about ten except that humans have ten fingers. Any whole number of 2 or more can be a base. A number system with base r uses the digits 0 to r − 1, and the position values are powers of r. To avoid confusion when several bases appear on a page, the base is written as a small subscript: 10112 is binary, 101110 is decimal (one thousand and eleven).

    The binary system: bits, bytes and words

    A binary digit is called a bit (short for binary digit). A bit has only two possible values, 0 and 1. In a circuit these might be represented by 0 V and 3.3 V, by a transistor that is off or on, by a tiny magnetic region pointing one way or the other on a hard disk, or by a pit or no pit on a CD. Because a circuit only has to decide between two clearly different states, it is very tolerant of small errors and noise (this is the main reason computers use binary; see 1.9 Analogue and digital signals).

    The leftmost bit has the largest place value and is called the most significant bit (MSB). The rightmost bit, worth 1, is the least significant bit (LSB). In digital audio the LSB is the smallest voltage step the converter can resolve, so "LSB" is also used to mean the size of one step (see 1.10).

    DefinitionStorage sizes use prefixes. In the SI system 1 kilobyte (kB) = 1000 bytes and 1 megabyte (MB) = 1 000 000 bytes. Computers often count in powers of 2, so 1 kibibyte (KiB) = 1024 bytes and 1 mebibyte (MiB) = 1 048 576 bytes. Operating systems do not all use the same convention, which is why a "500 GB" disk may show as about 465 "GB" on some computers.

    Converting decimal to binary: repeated division by 2

    The standard method for whole numbers is repeated division by 2 (sometimes called the double-dabble or remainder method):

    1. Divide the decimal number by 2. Write down the whole-number quotient and the remainder (which is always 0 or 1).
    2. Divide the quotient by 2 again, and write down the new quotient and remainder.
    3. Repeat until the quotient becomes 0.
    4. Read the remainders from the last one to the first (bottom to top). That string of remainders is the binary number. The first remainder is the LSB; the last is the MSB.

    Why it works: dividing by 2 and keeping the remainder tells you whether the number is odd (remainder 1, so the 1s bit is 1) or even (remainder 0). Dividing the quotient by 2 again asks the same question about the 2s place, and so on up the place values.

For 37: 32 fits (1, leaves 5); 16 does not (0); 8 does not (0); 4 fits (1, leaves 1); 2 does not (0); 1 fits (1, leaves 0). Result 1001012 = 3710. Both methods always give the same answer.

    Fractions in binary (brief)

    Places to the right of the binary point are worth one half, one quarter, one eighth and so on, just as places after a decimal point are worth tenths and hundredths.

    0.b1 b2 b3 ... = b1×2⁻¹ + b2×2⁻² + b3×2⁻³ + ... = b1×0.5 + b2×0.25 + b3×0.125 + ...

    Binary fraction to decimal: 0.1012 = 0.5 + 0 + 0.125 = 0.625. And 1010.112 = 8 + 2 + 0.5 + 0.25 = 10.75.

    Decimal fraction to binary uses repeated multiplication by 2: multiply the fraction by 2, write down the whole-number part (0 or 1) as the next bit, keep only the fractional part, and repeat. Read the bits from top to bottom (first bit is the one just after the point).

    Worked example 7: 0.62510. 0.625 × 2 = 1.25 (bit 1, keep 0.25); 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 (bit 0); 0.5 × 2 = 1.0 (bit 1, remainder 0, stop). So 0.62510 = 0.1012. For 10.75, convert 10 and 0.75 separately: 1010 and .11, giving 1010.112.

    Worked example 8: 0.110. 0.1 → 0.2 (0) → 0.4 (0) → 0.8 (0) → 1.6 (1, keep 0.6) → 1.2 (1, keep 0.2) → 0.4 (0) → ... The pattern 0011 repeats forever: 0.110 = 0.000110011 0011...2. A simple decimal like 0.1 cannot be stored exactly in binary, which is why a computer sometimes shows 0.30000000000000004 instead of 0.3. This does not matter for audiometry, but it explains small rounding differences between software packages.

    Negative numbers and audio samples

    A sound wave swings above and below the resting pressure, so an audio sample needs both positive and negative values. Digital audio stores signed numbers using a scheme called two's complement, in which the MSB acts as a sign bit (0 for positive, 1 for negative). You do not need the details at this level; the important fact is the range. A 16-bit signed sample runs from −32 768 to +32 767 (that is, −215 to 215 − 1), still 65 536 different values in total. When you open a WAV file in MATLAB or Python the samples are often divided by 32 768 so that they lie between −1 and +1.

    Why computers use binary

    • Two states are easy to make with transistors: fully off or fully on. The circuit does not need to hold an exact in-between voltage.
    • Two states are easy to tell apart even with noise, temperature drift and ageing. A voltage of 2.9 V or 3.1 V is still read as "1" on a 3.3 V logic chip.
    • Two states suit many storage media: magnetised north or south, charge stored or not in a flash memory cell, light reflected or not on an optical disc.
    • Arithmetic and logic are simple: binary addition needs only the rules 0 + 0 = 0, 0 + 1 = 1, 1 + 1 = 10 (write 0, carry 1). For example 0101 + 0011 = 1000 (5 + 3 = 8). Logic gates (AND, OR, NOT) built from a few transistors do all the work.
    • Copies are perfect: a copied file has exactly the same bits as the original, so a recorded speech sample does not degrade after many copies.

    Bits and dynamic range: the 6 dB per bit rule

    This is the link between number systems and audio. When a sound is digitised (topic 1.10), each sample is stored as an n-bit number. The largest signal the converter can represent is set by its full-scale voltage. The smallest change it can represent is one step (one LSB). The ratio of largest to smallest is about 2n. Expressed in decibels (as a voltage or pressure ratio, 20 log10):

    Dynamic range ≈ 20 log10(2^n) = n × 20 log10(2) ≈ 6.02 × n dB where n is the number of bits. Each extra bit doubles the number of levels and adds about 6 dB.

    A more exact figure for a full-scale sine wave, the signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio, is 6.02n + 1.76 dB (derived in 1.10). Typical results:

    • Digital potentiometers (covered earlier in this unit) and volume controls in hearing aids use binary codes, for example a 7-bit volume control with 128 steps.

    Analogue signal and digital signal: representation and comparison

    B.Sc. B2.41 hourUnit 1

    The voltage coming out of a microphone is an analogue signal: it changes smoothly and can take any value. The same speech saved as a WAV file on a computer is a digital signal: a list of numbers taken at fixed instants. This topic explains how each kind of signal is represented, how they differ, and why nearly all modern clinical equipment converts sound to digital form as early as possible.

    What it is

    A signal is a quantity that changes with time and carries information. An analogue signal is continuous in time and in amplitude. A digital signal exists only at separate instants and can take only a limited set of values, each written as a binary number.

    Why it is required

    Audiologists and speech-language pathologists record, measure, store, send and process signals every day. Knowing which parts of the chain are analogue and which are digital explains noise, hum, clipping, file sizes and the quality of tele-practice audio.

    How it works

    Sound is analogue by nature. A transducer turns it into an analogue voltage, an analogue-to-digital converter samples and quantises that voltage into numbers, and a digital-to-analogue converter turns numbers back into a voltage to drive an earphone or loudspeaker.

    Background: what is a signal?

    A signal is any physical quantity that varies in a way that carries information. In speech and hearing work the most important signals are:

    • Acoustic signals: tiny fluctuations of air pressure above and below atmospheric pressure (sound).
    • Electrical signals: voltages or currents, for example the output of a microphone, the drive to a headphone, or the electrode voltages of an auditory brainstem response.
    • Mechanical signals: vibration of a bone vibrator, movement of the eardrum.
    • Digital data: numbers stored in memory or sent over a cable, Bluetooth or the internet.

    A signal is usually drawn as a graph with time on the horizontal axis and the value of the quantity (its amplitude, for example voltage) on the vertical axis. This is called the time-domain representation or waveform. Two properties of that graph decide whether a signal is analogue or digital: whether time is continuous or taken only at separate instants, and whether amplitude can take any value or only certain allowed levels.

    The analogue signal

    An analogue signal is continuous in time and continuous in amplitude. "Continuous in time" means it has a value at every instant, however closely you look. "Continuous in amplitude" means the value can be anything within its range, for example 0.1234567... V, with no fixed steps. The word analogue comes from "analogous": the voltage is an analogue, or likeness, of the sound pressure. When the pressure rises, the voltage rises in the same proportion.

    Examples in the clinic:

    • The voltage from a microphone in a sound level meter or a hearing aid (a few millivolts, following every ripple of the sound pressure).
    • The signal on the headphone wires of an audiometer during a pure-tone test.
    • The electrical activity picked up by electrodes in ABR testing before it is digitised.
    • Older recording media: vinyl discs (the groove shape is an analogue of the waveform) and cassette tapes (the magnetisation varies continuously).
    • A moving-needle meter such as the VU meter on an older speech audiometer.

    A sine wave, the pure tone of audiometry, is the simplest analogue signal:

    v(t) = A sin(2π f t) where v is the instantaneous voltage, A the peak amplitude (V), f the frequency (Hz) and t the time (s).

    The digital signal

    A digital signal is discrete in time and discrete in amplitude. "Discrete in time" means it is defined only at separate instants, usually equally spaced, called sampling instants. The spacing is the sampling interval Ts, and the number of samples per second is the sampling rate fs = 1/Ts. "Discrete in amplitude" means each value is rounded to one of a fixed set of levels, and each level is identified by a binary code (topic 1.8). A digital audio signal is therefore just a list of numbers, for example:

    x[n] = 0, 1420, 2811, 4145, 5396, 6536, ... (16-bit) where n = 0, 1, 2 ... is the sample number; the sample x[n] was taken at time t = n × Ts. These values are a 1 kHz tone sampled at 44.1 kHz with a peak of 10 000.

    When digital data travel along a wire, each bit is usually sent as one of two voltages, for example about 0 V for a 0 and about 3.3 V for a 1. The receiving circuit only has to decide "high" or "low". This two-level electrical waveform is also often called a digital signal.

    Examples: a WAV or MP3 file of a speech sample; the stream from a USB audio interface; the numbers inside a digital hearing aid's processor; the audio sent over a Bluetooth link to a hearing aid; the stored test results in an audiometer.

    Noise immunity: the main advantage of digital

    Every cable, amplifier, tape and radio link adds a little unwanted noise and distortion. In an analogue system this noise adds directly to the signal and cannot be separated from it afterwards, because any value is a legal value. Each further copy or stage adds more: a cassette copied from a copy sounds noticeably worse, and a long analogue telephone line hisses.

    In a digital system the receiver only needs to decide whether each bit is a 0 or a 1. As long as the noise is smaller than about half the gap between the two voltages, the decision is always correct and the original bits are recovered exactly. The signal can then be sent on as fresh, clean pulses. This is called regeneration. Digital data can therefore be copied, stored and transmitted any number of times without loss. Extra check bits (error-detecting and error-correcting codes) allow even occasional wrong decisions to be found and fixed, which is how a slightly scratched CD still plays perfectly.

    Analogue: advantages and disadvantages

    • Advantage: a natural, exact likeness of the sound with infinite resolution in principle.
    • Advantage: no conversion delay; useful where a few milliseconds matter.
    • Advantage: simple circuits for simple jobs (a basic amplifier or tone control).
    • Disadvantage: noise and distortion accumulate at every stage and every copy.
    • Disadvantage: components drift, so equipment needs frequent recalibration.
    • Disadvantage: complex processing (many-channel compression, feedback cancellation) is impractical.
    • Disadvantage: storage media wear out and are bulky.

    Digital: advantages and disadvantages

    • Advantage: high noise immunity, perfect copies, reliable long-term storage.
    • Advantage: flexible processing by software; one hardware platform can run many programs.
    • Advantage: easy to compress, encrypt, transmit over networks and link to records (tele-practice).
    • Advantage: stable, repeatable behaviour without drift.
    • Disadvantage: needs ADC and DAC; conversion adds quantisation noise and possible aliasing.
    • Disadvantage: processing takes time, adding a few milliseconds of delay in hearing aids.
    • Disadvantage: needs more bandwidth and storage than the analogue signal unless compressed.
    • Disadvantage: hard clipping at full scale sounds harsher than analogue overload.

    Converting analogue to digital and digital to analogue

    B.Sc. B2.42 hoursUnit 1

    An analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) turns the voltage from a microphone into a stream of binary numbers, and a digital-to-analogue converter (DAC) turns numbers back into a voltage for an earphone or loudspeaker. Every digital hearing aid, audiometer, speech analysis system and recording laptop contains both. This topic explains sampling, the Nyquist rule, aliasing, quantisation and bit depth, the common converter circuits, and how to work out the size of an audio file.

    What it is

    An ADC measures an analogue voltage at regular instants (sampling) and rounds each measurement to the nearest of 2n levels (quantisation), outputting an n-bit code. A DAC does the reverse: it produces a voltage proportional to each code and smooths the result.

    Why it is required

    Sound is analogue, but processing, storage and transmission are digital (topic 1.9). Converters are the bridge. Their sampling rate and bit depth set the highest frequency and the dynamic range the whole system can handle, so they limit the quality of every test signal and recording.

    How it works

    Filter out frequencies above half the sampling rate, hold the voltage steady for a moment, compare it with reference voltages to find the nearest level, and output the binary code. On the way out, a resistor network makes a voltage for each code and a low-pass filter removes the steps.

    Background: the three steps of conversion

    Converting an analogue signal to digital involves three operations, each of which you met in the four-panel figure of topic 1.9:

    1. Sampling: measuring the signal at equally spaced instants, making time discrete.
    2. Quantisation: rounding each measured value to the nearest allowed level, making amplitude discrete.
    3. Encoding: writing the number of each level as a binary code (topic 1.8).

    A useful analogy is a nurse charting a patient's temperature. The temperature changes continuously (analogue). The nurse measures it every four hours (sampling), reads the thermometer to the nearest 0.1 °C (quantisation) and writes the number on the chart (encoding). If the readings are too far apart, a short fever spike can be missed completely; if the thermometer only reads whole degrees, small changes are lost. Sampling rate and bit depth play exactly these roles for sound.

    Architecture and signal flow

    Figure 1.10a. The complete conversion chain, as found in a digital hearing aid or a computer recording and playback system. Amber blocks are filters; teal blocks are the converters.

    Walking through the chain:

    1. Microphone: converts sound pressure to a small voltage, typically millivolts for conversational speech.
    2. Preamplifier: raises the voltage to suit the ADC's input range (for example about ±1 V). Too little gain wastes bits; too much causes clipping.
    3. Anti-aliasing filter: a low-pass filter that removes any frequencies above half the sampling rate before sampling, so that they cannot create false tones (aliasing, explained below).
    4. Sample-and-hold (S/H): takes a snapshot of the voltage at each sampling instant and holds it constant while the ADC measures it.
    5. Quantiser and encoder: finds which of the 2n levels is nearest and outputs its n-bit code.
    6. DSP (digital signal processor): does the useful work on the numbers, such as amplification, filtering, compression or noise reduction. In a recorder it may simply store them.
    7. DAC: produces a voltage proportional to each code, held until the next code arrives, giving a staircase.
    8. Reconstruction filter: a low-pass filter that smooths the staircase into a continuous waveform by removing the high-frequency images created by the steps.
    9. Power amplifier and receiver: drive the earphone or loudspeaker to produce sound.

    Sampling and the Nyquist rule

    The sampling rate (or sampling frequency) fs is the number of samples taken per second, measured in hertz (Hz) or samples per second. The time between samples is the sampling interval Ts = 1/fs. At 44.1 kHz, Ts = 1/44 100 ≈ 22.7 µs; at 16 kHz, Ts = 62.5 µs.

    How fast must we sample? The answer is the sampling theorem (associated with Nyquist and Shannon): a signal containing no frequencies above fmax can be captured completely, and later rebuilt exactly, if it is sampled at more than twice that frequency.

    fs > 2 × fmax (sampling theorem) fN = fs / 2 (Nyquist frequency) where fs is the sampling rate, fmax the highest frequency present in the signal and fN the Nyquist frequency, the highest frequency a given fs can represent.

    Intuitively, a sine wave needs at least two samples per cycle, one near each peak and trough, for its frequency to be identified. In practice systems sample somewhat faster than twice the highest wanted frequency to leave room for a real anti-aliasing filter, which cannot cut off instantly.

    Aliasing

    If a signal contains a frequency above the Nyquist frequency, the samples do not simply miss it. They record it as a false, lower frequency, called an alias. Once aliased, the false tone is indistinguishable from a real one and cannot be removed. The familiar example is in films, where a fast-spinning wagon wheel or propeller seems to turn slowly or even backwards: the camera "samples" 24 or so pictures per second, too slowly for the real rotation.

    For a frequency f between fs/2 and fs, the alias appears at:

    f_alias = | f − k × fs | where k is the whole number that makes the result lie between 0 and fs/2. For f between fs/2 and fs, k = 1, so f_alias = fs − f.

    Worked example 1: a 7 kHz tone is sampled at 10 kHz. The Nyquist frequency is 5 kHz, so 7 kHz is too high. Alias = 10 − 7 = 3 kHz. After the DAC you would hear a 3 kHz tone that was never in the original.

    Worked example 2: a 30 kHz ultrasonic component (for example from some electronic equipment or a dog whistle) reaches a recorder sampling at 44.1 kHz with no filter. Alias = 44.1 − 30 = 14.1 kHz, which is clearly audible.

    Worked example 3: a 5 kHz tone sampled at 44.1 kHz. 5 kHz is below 22.05 kHz, so it is captured correctly, with 44.1/5 ≈ 8.8 samples per cycle.

    The anti-aliasing filter

    Since aliasing cannot be undone, it must be prevented. The anti-aliasing filter is an analogue low-pass filter placed before the sampler. It passes the wanted band (for example up to 20 kHz for CD) and strongly attenuates everything above the Nyquist frequency. For CD, the gap between 20 kHz and 22.05 kHz is small, so the filter must be steep. Modern converters make the job easier by first sampling much faster than needed (oversampling, see sigma-delta converters below), so a simple analogue filter suffices and a sharp digital filter finishes the job.

    Common errorStudents sometimes think that aliasing only affects frequencies we cannot hear. In fact an inaudible ultrasonic component can produce an audible alias, and a high speech harmonic can alias into the speech band and distort a spectrogram.

    Quantisation and bit depth

    After sampling, each held voltage must be expressed as one of a limited number of levels. An n-bit converter has 2n levels spread across its full-scale range. The spacing between levels is the step size or quantisation step (Δ, also called 1 LSB).

    Levels = 2^n Δ = full-scale range / 2^n Max error = ± Δ / 2 where n is the bit depth (number of bits per sample) and the full-scale range is, for example, 2 V for an input of ±1 V.

    Worked example 4: a 3-bit converter with range ±1 V has 8 levels, Δ = 2/8 = 0.25 V, and the largest rounding error is 0.125 V. A 16-bit converter with the same range has 65 536 levels, Δ = 2/65 536 ≈ 30.5 µV, and the largest error is about 15 µV.

    The difference between the true sample value and the rounded value is the quantisation error. For normal audio signals it behaves like a small random noise, called quantisation noise, spread evenly between −Δ/2 and +Δ/2 (its RMS value is Δ/√12). The ratio of a full-scale sine wave to this noise is the signal-to-quantisation-noise ratio (SQNR):

    SQNR ≈ 6.02 n + 1.76 dB (full-scale sine wave) where n is the number of bits. For 8 bits ≈ 49.9 dB, 16 bits ≈ 98.1 dB, 24 bits ≈ 146.2 dB.

    This is the "6 dB per bit" rule of topic 1.8. Two practical consequences follow. First, the SQNR is quoted for a full-scale signal; a signal recorded 30 dB below full scale gets only about 68 dB instead of 98 dB from a 16-bit converter, because the noise stays the same while the signal is smaller. Second, signals larger than full scale cannot be represented at all: the converter outputs its maximum code and the waveform is flattened, which is called clipping and sounds harsh.

    DefinitionDigital levels are often given in dBFS (decibels relative to full scale). 0 dBFS is the largest possible sample value; everything else is negative, for example −12 dBFS. Aim for speech peaks around −12 to −6 dBFS when recording, leaving headroom for unexpected loud sounds.

    For very quiet signals, quantisation error becomes correlated with the signal and sounds like a gritty distortion rather than noise. Adding a tiny amount of random noise before quantising, called dither, turns this distortion back into smooth, benign noise. Audio software applies dither automatically when reducing a recording from 24 or 32 bits to 16 bits.

    Sample-and-hold

    Most ADCs need a short but definite time to work out the code. If the input voltage changed during that time, the result would be wrong. A sample-and-hold circuit solves this. In its simplest form it is an electronic switch followed by a small capacitor and a buffer amplifier. During the brief "sample" phase the switch closes and the capacitor charges to the input voltage. During the "hold" phase the switch opens and the capacitor keeps that voltage steady while the ADC measures it. A good S/H must charge quickly (short acquisition time), leak very little during hold (low droop) and open at precisely the right moment (low aperture jitter).

    Types of ADC

    Flash (parallel) ADC

    A flash ADC compares the input with every level at once. A chain of equal resistors divides the reference voltage into 2n − 1 threshold voltages, and each threshold has its own comparator (a circuit that outputs 1 if its input is above the threshold). For an input of, say, 0.6 of full scale, all comparators whose thresholds are below 0.6 output 1 and the rest output 0, like the rising level in a thermometer. An encoder turns this "thermometer code" into binary. Flash converters are the fastest type (one step per conversion) but need many comparators: 7 for 3 bits, 255 for 8 bits. They are therefore used for fast, low-resolution tasks such as video and oscilloscopes, not for high-resolution audio.

    Successive approximation (SAR) ADC

    A SAR ADC works like the guessing game "I am thinking of a number between 0 and 15; is it 8 or more?". It contains a DAC, one comparator and a register. It first sets the MSB to 1 (half of full scale) and compares the DAC output with the held input. If the input is higher, the bit stays 1; otherwise it is cleared. It then tries the next bit, and so on down to the LSB. An n-bit conversion takes n steps.

    Worked example 5: a 4-bit SAR ADC with full scale 1.6 V (step 0.1 V) converts an input of 1.13 V.

    StepTrial codeDAC voltageInput ≥ DAC?Decision

    Result: 10112 = 11, representing 11 × 0.1 = 1.1 V (the error of 0.03 V is less than one step). SAR converters offer a good balance of speed, resolution (typically 8 to 18 bits) and low power, and are common in microcontrollers and measurement instruments.

    Sigma-delta (ΣΔ) ADC

    A sigma-delta converter takes a different approach. It samples extremely fast, many times the final rate (for example 64 times), but with a very coarse quantiser, often just 1 bit. A feedback loop continually compares the input with the running output and pushes the error away from the audio band to very high frequencies (noise shaping). A digital filter then removes this high-frequency noise and reduces the rate to the final value (decimation), producing high-resolution samples, typically 16 to 24 bits. Advantages: excellent resolution and linearity, very simple analogue anti-aliasing filter, low cost. Drawback: a few hundred microseconds to a few milliseconds of delay from the digital filtering. Nearly all audio ADCs and DACs, including those in hearing aids, sound cards and audio interfaces, are of the sigma-delta type.

    Digital-to-analogue conversion

    A DAC produces an output voltage proportional to the binary number at its input:

    Vout = Vref × D / 2^n where Vref is the reference voltage, D the decimal value of the input code and n the number of bits.

    Weighted-resistor DAC

    The simplest idea gives each bit a resistor whose value is inversely proportional to the bit's weight: R for the MSB, 2R for the next, 4R, 8R and so on. Each bit switch connects its resistor to Vref (bit = 1) or to 0 V (bit = 0). The currents through the resistors add at the input of an op-amp summing amplifier, whose output voltage is proportional to the total current and hence to the binary number. The problem is precision: an 8-bit DAC needs resistors from R to 128R, and a 16-bit one needs a ratio of 32 768 to 1, all accurate to better than one LSB. Such a wide range of precise resistors is very hard to make on a chip.

    R-2R ladder DAC

    The R-2R ladder solves this with only two resistor values, R and 2R, repeated for each bit (Figure 1.10b). Because of the way the ladder is built, the resistance looking into any node towards the end is always 2R, so the current divides exactly in half at each node. Each bit therefore contributes half the current of the bit before it, giving the binary weights automatically. Only matching of R and 2R matters, which is easy on a chip.

    Figure 1.10b. A 4-bit R-2R ladder DAC (simplified). Only two resistor values are needed however many bits are used.

    Worked example 6: a 4-bit DAC with Vref = 5 V receives the code 1011. D = 8 + 2 + 1 = 11. Vout = 5 × 11/16 = 3.4375 V. One LSB = 5/16 = 0.3125 V, and the maximum output (code 1111) is 5 × 15/16 = 4.6875 V. A weighted-resistor DAC with Rf = R/2 would give the same magnitude (with a minus sign, because the summing amplifier inverts).

    Audio DACs today are mostly sigma-delta designs, the mirror image of the sigma-delta ADC, but the ladder DAC remains the clearest way to understand how a number becomes a voltage, and ladder DACs are used inside SAR ADCs.

    The reconstruction filter

    The DAC holds each output value until the next sample, producing a staircase (sometimes called a zero-order hold). The staircase contains the wanted signal plus unwanted high-frequency copies, called images, around multiples of the sampling rate; these give the steps their sharp edges. The reconstruction filter (also called anti-imaging or smoothing filter) is a low-pass filter after the DAC that removes everything above fs/2, leaving a smooth waveform. It is the output-side twin of the anti-aliasing filter. In hearing aids the receiver's own limited high-frequency response helps with this smoothing.

    File size of digital audio

    Uncompressed audio (WAV, AIFF) stores every sample. The data rate and file size follow directly:

    Bit rate (bit/s) = fs × n × channels File size (bytes) = fs × n × channels × duration / 8 where fs is the sampling rate (Hz), n the bit depth, channels 1 for mono or 2 for stereo, duration in seconds; divide by 8 to convert bits to bytes. A WAV file adds a small header of a few tens of bytes.

    Worked example 7 (CD audio, 44.1 kHz, 16-bit, stereo): bit rate = 44 100 × 16 × 2 = 1 411 200 bit/s ≈ 1411 kbit/s. One minute: 1 411 200 × 60 / 8 = 10 584 000 bytes ≈ 10.6 MB (about 10.1 MiB). A 74-minute CD therefore holds roughly 780 MB of audio.

    Worked example 8 (speech sample): a 3-minute mono recording at 16 kHz and 16 bits. Size = 16 000 × 16 × 1 × 180 / 8 = 5 760 000 bytes ≈ 5.76 MB.

    Worked example 9 (high-quality voice recording): 1 minute mono at 48 kHz and 24 bits. Size = 48 000 × 24 × 60 / 8 = 8 640 000 bytes ≈ 8.64 MB.

    Compressed formats such as MP3 or AAC reduce size roughly five to ten times or more by discarding detail judged inaudible. They are fine for listening, but for acoustic analysis (jitter, shimmer, formants, spectral measures) always keep an uncompressed original, because compression alters exactly the fine detail being measured.

    Exam tipIn file-size questions, write the formula, substitute with units, and remember the division by 8 for bytes and whether the question means mono or stereo.

    The CD standard: 44.1 kHz and 16 bits

    The compact disc, standardised around 1980, stores stereo audio at 44.1 kHz and 16 bits. The sampling rate was chosen to exceed twice the 20 kHz limit of human hearing (Nyquist frequency 22.05 kHz), leaving a 2.05 kHz band for the anti-aliasing filter to roll off; the exact figure came from the video-tape equipment used to master early digital recordings. The 16-bit depth gives about 96 dB of dynamic range, far more than vinyl or tape. The CD format also includes powerful error-correction codes, which is why small scratches do not affect playback. Many clinical speech test materials were first distributed on CD at this format.

    Clinical applications

    • Digital hearing aids: the microphone signal is converted at typically 16 to 33 kHz with 16 or more bits; the DSP applies frequency-specific gain, compression and noise reduction; the DAC drives the receiver. The ADC's maximum input level decides whether loud music or the client's own voice is captured without distortion.
    • Audiometers: tones and speech are generated digitally and converted by a DAC; the bit depth and output stage must give a clean signal across the full range of attenuator settings, from −10 dB HL to 120 dB HL at some frequencies.
    • Speech and voice analysis: the sampling rate must cover the frequencies of interest (for example up to 10 kHz for fricatives) and the bit depth must allow soft voices to be recorded without excessive quantisation noise.
    • Evoked potentials (ABR, OAE): tiny microvolt signals are amplified, filtered and digitised at high sampling rates; averaging many sweeps relies on accurate, repeatable conversion.
    • Tele-audiology: audio is digitised, compressed and sent over networks; knowing bit rates helps choose settings for limited bandwidth.

    Current developments

    • Hearing-aid converters are extending input range so that loud music and the wearer's own voice are captured without clipping, using higher-resolution or dual-range ADC designs.
    • Sampling rates and bandwidths in hearing aids have increased over the years to reproduce more high-frequency speech information.
    • Digital microphones with a built-in sigma-delta converter place the ADC inside the microphone package, shortening the analogue path.
    • Very low-power, low-latency converters are being developed for hearables and implantable devices, where every microwatt and microsecond matters.

    Key points to remember

    • A/D conversion = sampling (discrete time) + quantisation (discrete amplitude) + encoding (binary code).
    • Sampling theorem: fs must be more than twice the highest frequency; fs/2 is the Nyquist frequency.
    • Frequencies above fs/2 fold back as false tones (aliases); for f between fs/2 and fs, f_alias = fs − f.
    • The anti-aliasing low-pass filter before the ADC prevents aliasing; aliasing cannot be removed afterwards.
    • n bits give 2n levels; step Δ = range/2n; maximum rounding error Δ/2.
    • SQNR ≈ 6.02n + 1.76 dB: 16 bits ≈ 98 dB; about 6 dB per bit.
    • Sample-and-hold keeps the voltage steady during conversion.
    • ADC types: flash (fastest, many comparators), SAR (binary search, n steps), sigma-delta (oversampling, used for audio).
    • DACs: weighted resistor (needs a wide range of values), R-2R ladder (only R and 2R); a reconstruction filter smooths the output.
    • CD: 44.1 kHz, 16 bits, stereo = 1411.2 kbit/s, about 10.6 MB per minute.
