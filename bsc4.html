    Computer hardware: processor, motherboard, hard disk and RAM

    B.Sc. B2.41.5 hoursUnit 4

    Nearly every instrument in a speech and hearing clinic is either a computer or is run from one: the diagnostic audiometer, the hearing-aid fitting station, the speech analysis workstation and the patient record system. This topic opens the box and explains what each main part does, how the parts talk to each other and why some parts are fast and small while others are slow and large.

    What it is

    A computer is an electronic machine that accepts data (input), processes it according to a list of instructions called a program, stores data and results, and presents the results (output). The physical parts you can touch are the hardware; the programs are the software.

    Why it is required

    Knowing the hardware helps you choose a suitable PC for clinical software, understand error messages such as "low memory" or "disk full", protect patient data from disk failure, and describe problems accurately to an engineer or supplier.

    How it works

    Programs and data are copied from long-term storage (hard disk or SSD) into fast working memory (RAM). The processor fetches instructions from RAM one after another, carries them out on the data and writes back results. All of these parts sit on, or plug into, the motherboard, which connects them with buses.

    Background: what a computer does

    Every computer, from a hearing aid's tiny chip to a laptop, follows the same basic cycle described by the mathematician John von Neumann in the 1940s. Instructions and data are both kept in the same memory as numbers. The processor repeatedly:

    1. Fetches the next instruction from memory.
    2. Decodes it, which means working out what operation is being asked for (add, compare, move data, jump to another instruction).
    3. Executes it, for example adds two numbers in the arithmetic unit.
    4. Stores the result back in a register or in memory, then moves on to the next instruction.

    This is called the fetch, decode, execute cycle, and a modern processor performs billions of these cycles per second. Everything inside a computer is represented in binary: a bit is a single 0 or 1, and 8 bits make a byte. One byte can hold a number from 0 to 255 or one English letter. Larger units are kilobyte (kB, 1000 bytes), megabyte (MB, one million bytes), gigabyte (GB, 109 bytes) and terabyte (TB, 1012 bytes).

    DefinitionStorage makers use decimal units (1 GB = 1 000 000 000 bytes) while operating systems such as Windows often count in powers of two (1 GiB = 1 073 741 824 bytes). This is why a "1 TB" drive shows as about 931 GB in Windows. Nothing is missing.

    Walking through the diagram:

    • Input devices turn the outside world into data: a keyboard gives key codes, a microphone with a sound card gives sampled sound, and a USB audiometer sends button presses and settings.
    • The CPU (central processing unit, or processor) is the part that carries out instructions. Its control unit fetches and decodes instructions and directs traffic; the arithmetic logic unit (ALU) does calculations and comparisons; registers are a handful of tiny storage locations inside the CPU that hold the numbers being worked on right now. Cache is a small, very fast memory on the processor chip that keeps copies of recently used data from RAM.
    • Main memory (RAM) holds the operating system, the programs that are open and the data they are using, for example the sound file you have open in Praat.
    • Storage (hard disk drive or solid-state drive) keeps everything permanently: programs, the operating system and patient files.
    • Output devices show or play the results: the monitor, a printer for the audiogram report, speakers or calibrated earphones.

    The processor (CPU)

    The processor is a single silicon chip containing billions of transistors, each acting as a tiny electronic switch (transistors are introduced with amplifiers in Unit 1). It sits in a socket on the motherboard (desktops) or is soldered to it (most laptops), under a metal heat spreader and a cooler, because it turns electrical power into heat.

    Clock speed

    The processor's work is paced by a clock: a crystal-controlled square wave. Each tick is one clock cycle. Clock frequency is measured in gigahertz (GHz): 1 GHz is one thousand million cycles per second. A 4 GHz processor has a cycle time of 1 / (4 × 109) = 0.25 ns. Current desktop and laptop processors typically run at a base clock of about 1 to 3.5 GHz and can raise it briefly to about 4 to 5.5 GHz (called boost or turbo) when cooling and power allow. Clock speed alone does not decide performance: a newer design may complete more instructions per clock than an older one at the same GHz.

    Cores and threads

    A core is a complete processing unit (control unit, ALU, registers, L1 cache). A modern processor has several cores on one chip, so it can run several programs, or several parts of one program, at the same time. Typical 2025 to 2026 laptop and desktop processors have about 6 to 16 cores; budget ones may have 4. Many designs mix large performance cores with smaller efficiency cores that save battery on light tasks. A thread is one stream of instructions; some cores can run two threads at once (Intel calls this Hyper-Threading, AMD calls it simultaneous multithreading, SMT).

    Cache

    RAM is too slow to keep up with the processor, so the CPU keeps recently used data in cache memory built on the same chip. There are usually three levels: L1 (smallest and fastest, private to each core, tens of kilobytes), L2 (larger, around 1 to 3 MB per core) and L3 (shared by all cores, typically about 8 to 36 MB). When the processor finds what it needs in cache (a cache hit) it avoids a slow trip to RAM.

    Other parts on the processor chip

    • Integrated graphics (iGPU): draws the screen. Enough for clinical software, office work and video calls.
    • Memory controller: talks to the RAM.
    • NPU (neural processing unit): many processors released from 2024 onwards include a small accelerator for artificial-intelligence tasks such as background blur in video calls, live captions and noise suppression. Microsoft's "Copilot+ PC" label, for example, has required an NPU rated at 40 TOPS (trillion operations per second) or more together with at least 16 GB of RAM.

    The main processor families in personal computers today are Intel Core and Core Ultra, AMD Ryzen, Apple M-series (in Macs) and Qualcomm Snapdragon X (in some Windows laptops). Intel and AMD processors use the x86-64 instruction set; Apple and Qualcomm chips use the ARM instruction set, which matters because older Windows programs and device drivers written for x86 may not run, or may run only through translation, on ARM laptops.

    Clinical linkHearing-aid fitting software and USB audiometer drivers are usually written for Windows on x86-64 processors. Before buying a Windows laptop with an ARM processor, or a Mac, confirm with each manufacturer that their software and hardware interfaces are supported on it.

    The motherboard (also called the mainboard or system board) is the large printed circuit board that everything connects to. It carries power to each part and provides the electrical paths, called buses, along which data travels.

    Buses

    A bus is a set of conductors that carries data between parts, together with the rules (protocol) for using it. Classically a computer bus has three groups of lines: the data bus carries the actual numbers, the address bus says which memory location or device is being accessed, and the control bus carries signals such as read, write and clock. In a modern PC the main buses are:

    • Memory bus: CPU to RAM. For DDR5 memory each 64-bit channel moves data at 4800 to 6400 million transfers per second or more.
    • PCI Express (PCIe): fast serial links made of lanes. Graphics cards use 16 lanes (x16) and NVMe SSDs use 4 lanes (x4). Each new generation (PCIe 3.0, 4.0, 5.0) roughly doubles the speed per lane.
    • SATA: an older link for disks, limited to about 600 MB/s.
    • USB: the external bus for keyboards, audiometers, hearing-aid programming interfaces and memory sticks (details in 4.2).
    Peak bandwidth = transfers per second × bytes per transfer DDR5-5600, one channel: 5600 × 10^6 × 8 B = 44.8 GB/s where one 64-bit channel moves 8 bytes per transfer. Two channels (two modules in the right slots) give about 89.6 GB/s.

    Storage: hard disk drive and solid-state drive

    Hard disk drive (HDD): working principle

    A hard disk stores data magnetically. Inside a sealed metal case are one or more rigid platters, discs of aluminium or glass coated with a thin magnetic layer. A motor spins the platters at a constant speed, typically 5400 or 7200 revolutions per minute (rpm). A read/write head on the end of an actuator arm floats a few nanometres above the spinning surface on a cushion of air.

    • Writing: current through a tiny coil in the head creates a magnetic field that magnetises a small region of the platter in one of two directions, representing 0 or 1. This uses the electromagnetism you met with inductors in Unit 1.
    • Reading: as magnetised regions pass under the head, a sensor detects the changing magnetic field and converts it to a voltage.
    • The surface is organised into concentric tracks, each divided into sectors (typically 4096 bytes). To reach data the arm must move to the right track (seek time, several milliseconds) and wait for the sector to rotate under the head (rotational latency, on average half a revolution: 4.2 ms at 7200 rpm).

    Figure 4.1c. Left: inside a hard disk, a head on a moving arm reads a spinning magnetic platter. Right: an SSD has no moving parts; a controller reads and writes NAND flash chips electronically.

    Solid-state drive (SSD): working principle

    An SSD stores data in NAND flash memory chips. Each memory cell is a special transistor with an extra, electrically isolated floating gate (or a charge-trap layer in newer designs). Writing pushes electrons onto this gate through a thin insulator using a high voltage; the trapped charge stays there without power for years and shifts the voltage at which the transistor switches on. Reading applies a test voltage and checks whether the cell conducts. A cell can store 1 bit (SLC), 2 bits (MLC), 3 bits (TLC) or 4 bits (QLC) by using several charge levels; more bits per cell means cheaper but slower and less durable.

    A controller chip on the SSD spreads writes evenly across cells (wear levelling), because each cell survives only a limited number of erase cycles, and keeps a map of where each file's pieces really are. SSDs connect by SATA (2.5 inch box, up to about 550 MB/s) or, more commonly now, as an M.2 NVMe card using PCIe lanes (typically 3000 to 7000 MB/s for PCIe 4.0 drives, higher for PCIe 5.0).

    RAM and ROM

    RAM (random-access memory) is the computer's working memory. "Random access" means any location can be read in the same short time, unlike a tape that must be wound. PC RAM is DRAM (dynamic RAM): each bit is stored as charge on a tiny capacitor (the capacitor idea from Unit 1) with one transistor as a switch. The charge leaks away within milliseconds, so the memory controller must refresh every cell many times per second. When power is removed, the contents are lost: RAM is volatile. That is why unsaved work disappears in a power cut and why a UPS matters (Unit 1). Cache memory uses SRAM (static RAM), built from six transistors per bit, which is faster, needs no refresh, but is larger and costlier per bit.

    Current PCs use DDR5 modules (DDR4 in older ones); thin laptops often use LPDDR5 or LPDDR5X, low-power memory soldered to the board that cannot be upgraded later. The amount of RAM decides how many programs and how much data can be open at once. When RAM runs out the operating system moves some contents to the much slower SSD or HDD (called paging or swapping), and everything becomes sluggish.

    ROM (read-only memory) keeps its contents without power: it is non-volatile. Early ROM was written once at the factory. Today the firmware of a PC, an audiometer or a hearing aid lives in flash memory, a rewritable kind of non-volatile memory, so it can be updated. Related types are PROM (programmable once), EPROM (erasable with ultraviolet light) and EEPROM (erasable electrically, byte by byte).

    RAM

    • Volatile: loses data when power is off
    • Read and write, very fast (tens of nanoseconds)
    • Holds the OS, open programs and working data
    • Capacity in PCs typically 8 to 64 GB
    • DRAM: capacitor per bit, needs refresh

    ROM (and flash)

    • Non-volatile: keeps data without power
    • Mostly read; writing is slow or impossible
    • Holds firmware: start-up code, device programs
    • Capacity from kilobytes to a few megabytes for firmware
    • Found in PCs, audiometers, hearing aids, remote controls

    Memory hierarchy

    No single memory technology is fast, large, cheap and non-volatile all at once. Computers therefore use a memory hierarchy: a small amount of very fast, expensive memory close to the processor, backed by larger, slower, cheaper layers further away. The hierarchy works because programs tend to reuse the same data and nearby data (called locality of reference), so keeping a copy close by pays off.

    What happens when you switch on: the boot sequence

    1. Power button pressed: the power supply unit (PSU) starts and, once its output voltages are stable, sends a "power good" signal to the motherboard.
    2. The CPU starts executing the firmware (UEFI, older PCs: BIOS) stored in flash memory on the motherboard.
    3. POST (power-on self-test): the firmware checks the processor, RAM and essential devices. Faults are reported by beep codes or indicator lights.
    4. The firmware finds a boot device (SSD, HDD or USB stick) in the order set in its settings. With Secure Boot enabled it checks that the start-up code is digitally signed.
    5. A boot loader on that device loads the operating system kernel (its core) from storage into RAM.
    6. The kernel starts, loads device drivers (display, network, USB, audio) and system services.
    7. The login screen appears; after login, start-up programs load. The PC is ready.
    Common error"No bootable device" usually means the storage drive is not detected (loose cable, failed drive) or the boot order is wrong. "Operating system not found" after moving a drive to another PC often means a UEFI and legacy boot mode mismatch.

    Current developments

    • Processors with built-in NPUs for on-device AI (captioning, noise suppression, transcription) are becoming standard in new laptops.
    • ARM-based laptops are growing in the Windows market, raising compatibility questions for older clinical drivers.
    • DDR5 and soldered LPDDR5X memory are standard in new machines; because soldered memory cannot be upgraded, buying enough RAM at purchase matters more.
    • NVMe SSDs have largely replaced hard disks as the main drive; hard disks remain for bulk backup and archives.
    • Chiplet designs (several smaller dies in one package) let manufacturers mix cores, graphics and AI blocks flexibly.

    Key points to remember

    • A computer has input, processing (CPU), memory, storage and output units, linked by buses on the motherboard.
    • The CPU repeats the fetch, decode, execute cycle; its control unit directs, the ALU calculates, registers hold working values.
    • Performance depends on cores, clock frequency, instructions per clock and cache, not on GHz alone.
    • The motherboard carries the CPU socket, RAM slots, chipset, M.2 and SATA connectors, PCIe slots, firmware and CMOS battery.
    • HDDs store data magnetically on spinning platters; SSDs store charge in flash cells and have no moving parts.
    • RAM is fast volatile working memory; ROM and flash are non-volatile and hold firmware.
    • The memory hierarchy trades speed against size and cost: registers, cache, RAM, SSD, HDD.
    • For the clinic: SSD, enough RAM, good cooling and regular backups prevent most hardware trouble.

    Explain the working principle of a hard disk and a solid-state drive and compare them. Which would you choose for a PC in a sound-treated room, and why?

    Model answer: HDD: magnetised regions on spinning platters read and written by a head on a moving arm; seek and rotational delays of several ms. SSD: charge trapped in NAND flash cells managed by a controller; no moving parts, access below 0.1 ms. Compare speed, noise, shock resistance, cost per TB and failure behaviour. Choose an SSD: silent (no added room noise), faster and more robust, with a separate backup drive for safety.

    A 2 GB folder of speech recordings is copied from a SATA SSD reading at 500 MB/s. How long does the copy take if the destination can keep up? How long from a hard disk at 125 MB/s?

    Model answer: 2000 MB / 500 MB/s = 4 s; 2000 MB / 125 MB/s = 16 s. (Many small files take longer than these ideal figures.)

    What is a memory hierarchy? Why do computers use cache memory?

    Model answer: an arrangement of memories from small, fast and costly (registers, cache) to large, slow and cheap (RAM, SSD, HDD). Cache keeps copies of recently used data close to the CPU; because programs reuse nearby data (locality of reference), most accesses hit the cache, so the average access time approaches cache speed while capacity approaches that of RAM.

    Specification of personal computers and laptops

    B.Sc. B2.41 hourUnit 4

    A specification sheet lists what a computer contains: processor, memory, storage, display, ports, wireless, battery and operating system. Reading it correctly lets you pick a machine that will run audiometry, hearing-aid fitting and speech analysis software smoothly for several years, without paying for features the clinic does not need.

    What it is

    A specification (spec) is a list of the measurable features of a product. For a computer it names each hardware part and its capacity or speed, for example "16 GB DDR5 RAM" or "512 GB NVMe SSD".

    Why it is required

    Clinical software and hardware makers publish minimum requirements. A PC below them may not install the software, may fail to detect the audiometer or hearing-aid programmer, or may freeze in the middle of a session. Specs also decide battery life for outreach camps and how pleasant the machine is to use all day.

    How it works

    List the software and devices the PC must run, collect each one's minimum and recommended requirements, take the highest value for each item, add a margin for future updates, and then compare candidate machines line by line against that list.

    How to read a spec sheet

    A typical laptop listing might read: Core i5-1335U / 16 GB DDR4-3200 / 512 GB PCIe NVMe SSD / 15.6" FHD IPS 250 nits / Intel Iris Xe / Wi-Fi 6, BT 5.3 / 2x USB-A 3.2 Gen 1, 1x USB-C, HDMI 1.4 / 54 Wh / Windows 11 Home / 1.7 kg. Taking each item in turn:

    Processor

    The model name carries a code. In Core i5-1335U, "i5" is the performance tier (i3 entry, i5 mainstream, i7 and i9 higher), "13" is the generation, "35" the model within that generation, and the suffix "U" means a low-power laptop chip (about 15 W). Other common suffixes: H or HX for high-performance laptops, no suffix or K for desktops. Newer Intel chips are named Core Ultra 5, 7 and 9; AMD uses Ryzen 3, 5, 7 and 9 with similar meaning, and Apple uses M-series names. Because naming schemes change often, compare actual core counts, generation and independent benchmark results rather than the tier label alone. See 4.1 for cores, clock and cache.

    Memory (RAM)

    Look for three things: size (GB), type and speed (DDR4-3200, DDR5-5600, LPDDR5X-7500), and whether it is soldered or in slots. 8 GB is now the bare minimum for Windows 11 with a browser and office work; 16 GB is the sensible standard for clinical use in 2025 to 2026; 32 GB helps for long high-resolution recordings, MATLAB work or virtual machines. If the memory is soldered, buy the size you will need for the whole life of the machine.

    Storage

    Prefer an NVMe SSD of at least 512 GB. Speech recordings grow quickly: one minute of mono audio at 44.1 kHz, 16-bit takes about 5.3 MB, and stereo 48 kHz, 24-bit takes about 17.3 MB per minute. Video from teletherapy sessions is far larger. A hard disk as the only drive makes any modern PC feel slow.

    Display

    • Size is the diagonal in inches: laptops typically 13 to 16 inches, desktop monitors 22 to 27 inches.
    • Resolution is the number of pixels: FHD (Full HD) is 1920 × 1080, WUXGA 1920 × 1200, QHD 2560 × 1440, 4K 3840 × 2160. Spectrograms and audiograms with small text are easier to read at FHD or higher.
    • Panel type: IPS panels give good colour and viewing angles; TN panels are cheaper with poor angles; OLED gives deep blacks.
    • Brightness in nits (candela per square metre): about 250 nits is dim; 300 to 400 nits or more is comfortable in a bright room or outdoors at a camp.
    • Pixel density (PPI) = diagonal in pixels / diagonal in inches.
    PPI = √(W² + H²) / D 1920 × 1080 on 15.6 in: √(1920² + 1080²) / 15.6 = 2203 / 15.6 = about 141 pixels per inch where W and H are the horizontal and vertical pixel counts and D is the diagonal in inches.

    Graphics

    Integrated graphics (built into the processor) are enough for all clinical, office and video-call software. A separate (discrete) graphics card is needed only for gaming, heavy video editing, 3D work or training AI models.

    Battery and power

    Battery capacity is given in watt-hours (Wh). Run time is roughly capacity divided by average power drawn: a 60 Wh battery supplying an average of 10 W lasts about 6 hours. Real run time is shorter with a bright screen, video calls or a connected USB audiometer that takes power from the laptop. Many laptops now charge through USB-C Power Delivery (typically 45 to 100 W). Batteries lose capacity with age and heat; expect noticeable loss after two to four years.

    Audio

    Built-in speakers and microphones are designed for video calls, not measurement. They are uncalibrated, their frequency response is uneven and the operating system may apply noise suppression, echo cancellation and automatic gain that change the signal. Diagnostic audiometers contain their own calibrated audio hardware and must never rely on the PC's sound card unless the manufacturer specifies and calibrates that path.

    Operating systems: types, comparison and functioning

    B.Sc. B2.41.5 hoursUnit 4

    The operating system is the software layer that turns bare hardware into a usable computer. It starts the machine, shares the processor and memory among programs, stores files, drives devices such as audiometers and printers, and gives you a screen to work with. This topic explains the kinds of operating system, compares the ones you will meet in clinics and on phones, and describes the five main jobs every operating system does.

    What it is

    An operating system (OS) is system software that manages the computer's hardware and software resources and provides common services to application programs. Examples: Windows, macOS, Linux, Android, iOS.

    Why it is required

    Without an OS every program would have to contain its own code to handle the keyboard, the disk, the screen and the network, and two programs could not run safely at the same time. The OS does these jobs once, for everyone, and protects programs from each other.

    How it works

    At start-up the OS kernel is loaded into memory. Applications ask the kernel for services (open a file, send data to USB, get more memory) through system calls. The kernel uses device drivers to talk to each piece of hardware and a scheduler to share the processor.

    Where the operating system sits

    Figure 4.3a. Layers of a computer system. Applications never touch hardware directly; they ask the kernel, which uses drivers.

    Reading the layers from the top: users interact with applications through the user interface. When the application needs anything from the machine, it makes a system call to the kernel, the core part of the OS that always stays in memory and runs with full control of the hardware. The kernel relies on device drivers, small programs written for each hardware model, to operate the hardware. This layering means Praat does not need to know which make of sound card you have: it asks the OS to record, and the OS and driver deal with the details.

    Functions of an operating system

    1. Process management

    A program is a file on disk; a process is a program that is running, with its own memory and state. The OS creates and ends processes, and decides which one uses each processor core at each moment. This decision is CPU scheduling. A process moves between states: new, ready (waiting for the CPU), running, waiting (for input, disk or a device) and terminated. Switching the CPU from one process to another is a context switch: the OS saves the registers of one and loads those of the other, which takes a little time.

    Common scheduling methods:

    • First come, first served (FCFS): run jobs in arrival order until each finishes. Simple, but a long job makes short ones wait.
    • Shortest job first (SJF): run the shortest job first. Gives the lowest average waiting time, but needs to know job lengths.
    • Round robin (RR): each process gets a fixed time quantum (time slice), then goes to the back of the queue. Fair and responsive; used in time-sharing systems.
    • Priority scheduling: the most important process runs first. Desktop OSs give audio and user-interface threads high priority so that sound does not break up.
    Turnaround time = completion time - arrival time Waiting time = turnaround time - burst (CPU) time where burst time is the CPU time a process needs. With all processes arriving at time 0, turnaround equals completion time.

    Worked example: three processes arrive together: P1, an audiometer interface task, needs 3 ms; P2, a Praat analysis, needs 8 ms; P3, an antivirus scan, needs 5 ms. With FCFS in the order P1, P2, P3 they finish at 3, 11 and 16 ms, so waiting times are 0, 3 and 11 ms, average 4.67 ms. With SJF (P1, P3, P2) they finish at 3, 8 and 16 ms, waiting 0, 3 and 8 ms, average 3.67 ms. With round robin and a 2 ms quantum they finish at 7 (P1), 16 (P2) and 14 (P3) ms, waiting 4, 8 and 9 ms, average 7.0 ms, but every process gets the CPU within 4 ms of starting, which is what keeps a screen responsive.

    2. Memory management

    The OS keeps track of which parts of RAM are in use and by whom, gives memory to processes when they start and takes it back when they end. It also protects each process's memory so that one crashing program cannot overwrite another. Modern OSs use virtual memory: each process sees its own tidy address space, which the OS maps onto real RAM in fixed-size blocks called pages (typically 4 kB). When RAM is full, less-used pages are copied to a page file or swap space on the SSD and brought back when needed. This lets more programs run than would fit in RAM, at the cost of speed (see the memory hierarchy in 4.1).

    3. File management

    The OS organises storage into files (named collections of data such as client023_vowel_a.wav) and folders (directories) arranged as a tree. It records each file's name, size, dates, location on disk and access permissions in a file system (NTFS, APFS, ext4, or exFAT on memory cards). It provides operations to create, read, write, rename, copy and delete files, controls who may open them, and on journalling file systems keeps a log so that a power cut is less likely to corrupt the disk.

    4. Device (input/output) management

    The OS detects devices when they are connected (plug and play), loads the right driver, passes data between programs and devices, and uses buffers (temporary memory areas) to smooth out speed differences, for example collecting audio samples into blocks. It handles interrupts: signals from a device saying "I have data" or "I have finished", so the CPU does not have to keep checking. A spooler queues print jobs so you can keep working while the audiogram prints.

    5. User interface

    The OS gives the user a way to control the computer: a graphical user interface (GUI) with windows, icons, menus and a pointer (WIMP), a touch interface on phones and tablets, a command-line interface (CLI) where commands are typed (Command Prompt or PowerShell on Windows, Terminal on macOS and Linux), and increasingly voice control. Accessibility features are part of this layer: screen readers (Narrator, VoiceOver, TalkBack), captions, magnification, switch access and hearing-device settings are important for the clients we serve.

    Other functions

    • Security: user accounts and passwords, file permissions, firewall, disk encryption (BitLocker, FileVault), updates that fix vulnerabilities.
    • Networking: network drivers and protocols (TCP/IP) so programs can use the LAN and internet (4.6, 4.7).
    • Error detection and logging: event logs and crash reports help engineers find faults.
    • Resource accounting: tracking CPU, memory and battery use per program (Task Manager, Activity Monitor).

    Application software used in communication sciences and disorders

    B.Sc. B2.41 hourUnit 4

    Audiologists and speech-language pathologists use a wide range of programs: to test hearing, fit hearing aids, record and analyse speech and voice, support communication through AAC, deliver therapy, keep records and analyse research data. This topic groups these programs by purpose, explains what each group does and how to judge whether a program is suitable for clinical use.

    What it is

    Application software is a program written to help a user do a particular task, such as editing a report or measuring formant frequencies. It runs on top of the operating system (4.3), unlike system software, which runs the computer itself.

    Why it is required

    Modern assessment and management depend on software: audiometers store and print audiograms through it, hearing aids can only be programmed with it, and objective voice and speech measures come from it. Knowing what each program does and its limits makes you a safer and more efficient clinician.

    How it works

    The program takes input (a recording, button presses from an audiometer, a typed case history), processes it with algorithms (filtering, FFT, statistics, prescription formulas) and produces output (a graph, a number, a programmed device, a report), storing data in files or a database.

    System software and application software

    System software

    • Runs and manages the computer
    • Operating system, device drivers, firmware, utilities (antivirus, backup, disk tools)
    • Starts with the computer and runs in the background
    • Users rarely interact with it directly

    Application software

    • Does a job for the user
    • Office suites, Praat, NOAH, fitting software, AAC apps
    • Started by the user when needed
    • Depends on the system software to run

    Application software is distributed under different licences:

    • Proprietary (commercial): you buy a licence to use it; source code is secret. Example: MATLAB, SPSS, most clinical instrument software.
    • Freeware: free to use but not open. Example: many manufacturers' fitting programs are free to registered professionals.
    • Open-source: free, and the source code is published so anyone can inspect and improve it. Example: Audacity, R, GNU Octave. Praat is free and its source code is also available.
    • Subscription or cloud (software as a service, SaaS): runs in a web browser or app with a monthly or yearly fee; data is stored on the provider's servers. Example: online office suites, many practice-management systems.

    • Telepractice platforms: video-conferencing software (for example Zoom, Google Meet, Microsoft Teams) and government platforms such as eSanjeevani (see 4.8).
    • Learning management systems (Moodle, Google Classroom) for student teaching and parent training.

    Mobile apps: concept and functioning

    B.Sc. B2.41 hourUnit 4

    A smartphone is a small computer with a microphone, speaker, camera, touch screen, motion sensors and several radios. Apps use these parts to screen hearing, control and stream to hearing aids, provide AAC, give home practice and connect clients with clinicians. This topic explains what an app is, the kinds of app, how an app works with the phone's operating system and with cloud servers, and what to watch for in accuracy and privacy.

    What it is

    A mobile app (application) is a program designed to run on a mobile operating system such as Android or iOS, usually installed from an app store. Apps can also be web apps that open in the phone's browser.

    Why it is required

    Most of our clients carry a smartphone. Apps extend care beyond the clinic: hearing-aid adjustment at home, screening in the community, daily therapy practice, AAC anywhere, and reminders. Clinicians must be able to recommend suitable apps and explain their limits.

    How it works

    The app's code runs inside a protected area (a sandbox) on the phone. It asks the operating system, through programming interfaces (APIs), for access to the microphone, Bluetooth or storage, and only gets it if the user grants permission. Many apps also exchange data over the internet with a server in the cloud.

    App stores and installation

    Apps are distributed mainly through the Google Play Store (Android) and Apple App Store (iOS and iPadOS).     Walking through a typical interaction, such as changing a hearing-aid program from a phone:

    1. The user taps a button in the app's user interface.
    2. The app logic decides what to do: send the "program 2" command.
    3. It calls the operating system's Bluetooth API. The OS checks that the app has the Bluetooth permission and that the hearing aid is paired (a trusted, encrypted link set up earlier).
    4. The Bluetooth radio sends a short packet to the hearing aid, which changes program and sends back an acknowledgement.
    5. If the app has a cloud service, it may log the change on the server over an encrypted HTTPS connection (4.6) so that the audiologist can see usage data or send new settings remotely.

    Life cycle and background limits

    Mobile OSs manage apps strictly to save battery and memory. An app is launched, runs in the foreground while on screen, is moved to the background when you switch away, and may be suspended or closed by the OS at any time. Tasks such as continuous audio recording or Bluetooth connections in the background need special permissions and show a notification. This is why a screening app may stop if a call comes in, and why a hearing-aid app may disconnect after battery-saver settings close it.

    Hearing aids, implants and Bluetooth

    Hearing aids connect to phones by Bluetooth, a short-range radio in the 2.4 GHz band. Because hearing-aid batteries are tiny, most use Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE). Several approaches exist:

    • Made for iPhone (MFi) hearing aids: Apple's protocol for streaming audio and control from iPhones and iPads.
    • ASHA (Audio Streaming for Hearing Aids): Google's protocol for direct streaming on Android phones.
    • Classic Bluetooth: used by some hearing aids for universal streaming to most phones, at a higher battery cost.
    • Bluetooth LE Audio: the newer standard, with the efficient LC3 codec, a hearing-access profile for hearing aids, and Auracast broadcast audio, which lets a venue (lecture hall, airport, temple, TV) broadcast sound that any number of compatible hearing aids and earbuds can receive, like a modern wireless loop system. Support in phones, hearing aids and venues is growing through 2025 to 2026.

    Manufacturers' apps typically allow volume and program changes, tone adjustment, streaming control, battery status, usage logging, "find my hearing aid" and remote fine-tuning, in which the audiologist sends new settings through the cloud during a video appointment. Cochlear implant processors have similar apps. Some consumer earbuds now include a hearing test and a hearing-aid mode for adults with perceived mild to moderate loss, where regulators have authorised it.

    Hearing screening apps

    hearWHO is a free screening app from the World Health Organization for Android and iOS. It uses the digits-in-noise (DIN) method: 23 sets of three spoken digits are presented in background noise, the user types what they heard, and the noise level relative to the speech changes adaptively. The result is shown as a score from 0 to 100; WHO advises that a score below 50 suggests hearing loss needing professional assessment, 50 to 75 calls for monitoring, and above 75 indicates good hearing. It is meant for people aged 12 and over. Digits-in-noise tests work reasonably well on phones because they depend on the speech-to-noise ratio rather than on the absolute sound level, so calibration matters less than for pure-tone tests.

    Other apps perform pure-tone screening or audiometry. These are accurate only with the headphones they were calibrated with and in a quiet environment; some professional systems pair a tablet with calibrated headphones and noise monitoring and have published validation. Always treat a consumer app result as screening, not diagnosis.

    Structure of the internet and the World Wide Web

    B.Sc. B2.41 hourUnit 4

    When a clinician opens an online hearing-aid portal or joins a teletherapy call, data crosses many networks owned by different companies within a fraction of a second. This topic explains how the internet is built, how data finds its way in packets, what IP addresses and domain names are, and how the World Wide Web sits on top of the internet.

    What it is

    The internet is a worldwide network of networks: millions of computer networks connected together, all using a common set of rules called the TCP/IP protocols. The World Wide Web (WWW) is one service that runs on the internet: a collection of linked documents and applications (web pages) reached with a web browser.

    Why it is required

    Teleconsultation, cloud records, software updates, online journals, email and remote hearing-aid fitting all depend on the internet. Understanding its structure helps you explain slow or failed connections, choose a suitable connection for a clinic and recognise secure websites.

    How it works

    Data is split into small packets, each labelled with the sender's and receiver's IP addresses. Routers pass each packet from network to network towards its destination, where the packets are put back together. People use names such as www.who.int, which the Domain Name System (DNS) translates into IP addresses.

    Background: from a network to a network of networks

    A network is two or more computers connected so they can share data. A clinic's computers and printer connected by cables and Wi-Fi form a local area network (LAN, 4.7). Networks spread over a city or country are wide area networks (WANs). The internet grew from ARPANET, a US research network started in 1969, which adopted TCP/IP in 1983. No single organisation owns the internet; it works because every network agrees to use the same protocols and to exchange traffic with its neighbours.

    A protocol is an agreed set of rules for communication, like the rules of a conversation: who speaks, in what format, and what to do if a message is lost. The internet uses a stack of protocols in layers, each relying on the one below:

    Layer (TCP/IP model)JobExamples
    ApplicationWhat the user's program wants to doHTTP and HTTPS (web), DNS, SMTP and IMAP (email), video-call protocols
    TransportEnd-to-end delivery between programs; splits data into segments; TCP adds reliabilityTCP (reliable, ordered), UDP (fast, no resend; used for live audio and video)
    Internet (network)Addressing and routing packets across networksIP (IPv4, IPv6)
    Link and physicalMoving bits over one physical linkEthernet, Wi-Fi, 4G/5G, optical fibre

    Structure of the internet

    Figure 4.6a. The internet as a network of networks. Each organisation's network connects to an ISP; ISPs connect to backbone carriers and to each other.

    • End devices (hosts): laptops, phones, servers. Every host that talks on the internet needs an IP address.
    • Local network and router: the clinic's router joins the LAN to the ISP. It usually gives the LAN private addresses and shares one public address among them (network address translation, NAT).
    • Internet service provider (ISP): a company that sells internet access over fibre (FTTH), cable, DSL on telephone lines, mobile 4G and 5G, fixed wireless or satellite. Examples in India include BSNL, Airtel, Jio and many local fibre providers.
    • Backbone: very high capacity fibre networks run by large carriers, linked at internet exchange points (IXPs) where networks swap traffic. Countries are joined by submarine optical cables; India's cable landing stations are mainly in Mumbai and Chennai, with others on the coast including Kerala.
    • Servers and data centres: buildings full of computers that host websites, cloud storage, email and telehealth platforms.
    • Governance: technical standards come from bodies such as the IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force) and W3C (World Wide Web Consortium); ICANN coordinates domain names and IP address allocation.

    IP addresses

    An IP address identifies a device's connection on a network, like a postal address. IPv4 addresses are 32 bits long, written as four numbers from 0 to 255 separated by dots, for example 192.168.1.25. There are 232, about 4.3 billion, possible IPv4 addresses, which is not enough for all devices, so IPv6 uses 128 bits, written in hexadecimal groups such as 2001:db8::1, giving about 3.4 × 1038 addresses. India has one of the highest IPv6 adoption rates, largely because of mobile networks.

    • Public addresses are unique on the whole internet; private addresses (10.x.x.x, 172.16.x.x to 172.31.x.x, 192.168.x.x) are reused inside local networks.
    • Static addresses are fixed (servers, network printers); dynamic addresses are handed out automatically by a DHCP server (laptops, phones).
    • A port number identifies a program on a host: web traffic uses port 80 (HTTP) and 443 (HTTPS).

    Packets and routers: packet switching

    The internet uses packet switching. A file or a stream of audio is divided into packets, typically up to about 1500 bytes each on Ethernet and Wi-Fi. Each packet carries a header (source and destination IP addresses, sequence number, error check) and a payload (a piece of the data). A 5 MB audiogram report therefore travels as roughly 3400 packets.

    A router is a device that connects networks and forwards packets. It reads each packet's destination address, looks it up in its routing table and sends it out of the best link. Packets of one message may take different routes and arrive out of order; TCP at the receiver puts them back in order and asks for any missing ones to be resent. This design means the network keeps working when a link fails, because routers simply find another path. The older telephone network used circuit switching, which reserves one fixed path for the whole call.

    Delay = transmission time + propagation time + queueing Transmission time = data size (bits) / link rate (bit/s) Propagation time = distance / speed in medium (about 2 × 10^8 m/s in fibre) Example: a 1 MB file on a 10 Mbit/s link takes 8 Mbit / 10 Mbit/s = 0.8 s to send. Light in fibre from Thiruvananthapuram to Delhi (about 2300 km of cable) takes about 11.5 ms one way.

    The time for a packet to go to a server and for the reply to come back is the round-trip time (RTT) or latency, measured with the ping command. Bandwidth (more correctly, data rate or throughput) is how many bits per second a link carries. For a video call, both matter: bandwidth decides picture quality and latency decides how natural turn-taking feels.

    Domain Name System (DNS)

    People remember names, computers use numbers. The DNS is the internet's distributed directory that turns a domain name such as www.who.int into an IP address. Names are read right to left: .int is the top-level domain (TLD), who is the domain registered under it, and www names a host. Other TLDs include .com, .org, .in, .gov.in and .ac.in.

    1. Your browser checks its own cache and the operating system's cache.
    2. If not found, the computer asks a recursive DNS resolver, usually run by the ISP or a public service.
    3. If the resolver does not have the answer cached, it asks a root server, which refers it to the servers for the TLD (.int).
    4. The TLD servers refer it to the authoritative server for who.int.
    5. The authoritative server replies with the IP address; the resolver passes it back and caches it for a set time.

    Client, server and the World Wide Web

    Most internet applications follow the client-server model: a client (your browser or app) sends a request; a server (a program on a computer in a data centre) waits for requests and sends responses. The Web was invented by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN in 1989 to 1991 and rests on three ideas:

    • HTML (HyperText Markup Language): the language web pages are written in, with hyperlinks to other pages.
    • URL (Uniform Resource Locator): the address of a resource. In https://www.who.int/health-topics/hearing-loss, https is the scheme (protocol), www.who.int the host (domain), and /health-topics/hearing-loss the path to a particular page.
    • HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol): the request and response rules between browser and server. A browser sends GET /page; the server replies with a status code (200 OK, 404 Not Found, 500 Server Error) and the content.

    HTTPS is HTTP carried inside an encrypted TLS (Transport Layer Security) connection. It keeps the data private, prevents tampering and, through a digital certificate, proves the server really belongs to the domain shown. Browsers show a padlock or site information icon. Any page that handles logins or patient data must use HTTPS.

    A web browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari) is the client program that requests pages, then interprets HTML, applies CSS styles, runs JavaScript programs and displays the result. A search engine is a website that indexes pages; it is not the Web itself.

    Key points to remember

    • The internet is a network of networks using TCP/IP; the Web is a service on top of it.
    • Layers: application (HTTP, DNS), transport (TCP, UDP), internet (IP), link (Ethernet, Wi-Fi).
    • IPv4: 32-bit, about 4.3 billion addresses; IPv6: 128-bit.

    • DNS translates domain names to IP addresses via resolver, root, TLD and authoritative servers.
    • URL = scheme + host + path; HTTPS = HTTP inside TLS encryption.
    • Latency (RTT) and bandwidth are different; both matter for teleconsultation.

    Local area network: structure and components

    B.Sc. B2.41 hourUnit 4

    A local area network links the computers, instruments, printers and servers inside one building or campus so they can share patient records, files, printers and a single internet connection. This topic describes how LANs are laid out (topologies), the hardware they are built from, how devices get addresses, and how a typical speech and hearing clinic network is organised.

    What it is

    A local area network (LAN) is a computer network covering a limited area such as a room, a floor, a building or a campus, usually owned and managed by one organisation. Its devices are connected by cables (Ethernet) and radio (Wi-Fi).

    Why it is required

    Without a LAN each PC keeps its own copy of client files, results cannot be shared between the audiometry room and the hearing-aid fitting room, and every PC needs its own printer and internet line. A LAN makes shared, backed-up, access-controlled data possible.

    How it works

    Each device has a network interface card with a unique hardware (MAC) address. Devices plug into a switch, or join a Wi-Fi access point, which forwards data frames between them. A router connects the LAN to the internet. Each device receives an IP address, usually automatically.

    IP addressing in a LAN

    • Each device needs an IP address (4.6). A small LAN typically uses a private range such as 192.168.10.0/24.
    • The subnet mask (here 255.255.255.0, written /24) says which part of the address is the network (192.168.10) and which is the host (the last number). A /24 network has 256 addresses, of which 254 can be given to devices (the first identifies the network and the last is the broadcast address).
    • The default gateway is the router's address (for example 192.168.10.1): packets for anything outside the LAN go there.
    • DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol) on the router gives laptops and phones their address, mask, gateway and DNS server automatically.
    • Servers, printers and networked instruments should get fixed addresses (static, or a DHCP reservation) so other devices can always find them.
    • Larger sites divide the LAN into VLANs (virtual LANs): for example one for clinical devices, one for staff, one for guest Wi-Fi, so a visitor's phone can never reach patient records.
    Usable hosts in a subnet = 2^(32 - prefix length) - 2 /24: 2^8 - 2 = 254 /25: 2^7 - 2 = 126 where prefix length is the number of network bits (24 for mask 255.255.255.0).

    Tele-diagnosis and tele-rehabilitation

    B.Sc. B2.41 hourUnit 4

    Many people with hearing, speech and language disorders live far from a qualified audiologist or speech-language pathologist. Telepractice uses the computers, apps and networks of this unit to bring assessment and therapy to them. This topic explains the service models, what can and cannot be done remotely in audiology and speech-language pathology, the technology and bandwidth needed, and the rules on privacy and consent, with the Indian context.

    What it is

    Telehealth (or telemedicine) is the delivery of health services at a distance using information and communication technology. In our field it is often called telepractice. Tele-diagnosis is remote assessment (for example tele-audiometry or video otoscopy); tele-rehabilitation is remote intervention (for example speech therapy by video, remote hearing-aid fine-tuning, auditory training).

    Why it is required

    Specialists are concentrated in cities; travel is costly and tiring for children, older adults and people with disabilities; early identification and frequent therapy sessions improve outcomes. Telepractice widens access, reduces missed sessions and allows specialists to support local workers.

    How it works

    Information about the client (audio, video, images, test data) is captured at the client's site, sent over the internet securely, and reviewed by the clinician either live or later. The clinician's decisions, instructions or new device settings travel back the same way.

    Tele-diagnosis in audiology

    • Remote-controlled (synchronous) pure-tone audiometry: a calibrated audiometer at the client's site is controlled by the audiologist through remote desktop software or the manufacturer's tele-module. The facilitator fits the earphones; the audiologist sees the client by webcam and runs the test as usual. Studies generally report thresholds close to face-to-face testing when the equipment is calibrated and the environment is quiet enough.
    • Automated audiometry: the audiometer runs the test by itself (for example a computer-controlled Hughson-Westlake procedure), often with noise-attenuating headphones and ambient-noise monitoring; results are uploaded for review (store-and-forward).
    • Video otoscopy: a small camera otoscope captures still images or short videos of the ear canal and eardrum, which a specialist reviews for wax, perforation, otitis media or discharge.
    • Immittance, OAE and ABR: the facilitator places the probe or electrodes; tracings are either viewed live via remote control or uploaded. ABR needs more training at the remote end because electrode preparation affects quality.
    • Screening: newborn and school screening data (OAE pass or refer) uploaded to a central database; smartphone screening such as digits-in-noise (4.5).
    • Hearing aids and implants: remote fine-tuning over a manufacturer's app and cloud during a video call; remote cochlear implant programming is also used in some centres.
    SafetyRemote tests are only as valid as the equipment calibration and the test environment at the client's end. Check ambient noise, earphone placement and calibration records before trusting thresholds, and refer for in-person testing when results are doubtful, when masking is complex, or when medical signs such as ear discharge, sudden hearing loss or vertigo are present.

    Tele-rehabilitation in speech-language pathology and audiology

    • Tele-speech therapy: synchronous video sessions for articulation, language, fluency, voice and swallowing (screening and education only for swallowing unless trained support is present). Screen sharing lets the clinician show pictures, games and visual feedback; parents can be coached as the main agents of therapy at home.
    • Assessment by video: language samples, oral motor observation, standardised tests adapted with the publisher's permission, and recordings for analysis in Praat (4.4).
    • AAC support: remote editing of vocabulary files and coaching of communication partners.
    • Auditory training and counselling: live sessions or app-based programmes with remote monitoring of progress.
    • Parent and teacher training: group video sessions and recorded material.
    Common errorVideo-call software is designed to make speech intelligible, not accurate: it applies noise suppression, echo cancellation, automatic gain and strong compression, and may remove quiet or high-frequency sounds such as /s/. Voice quality, fricatives and loudness heard over a call can therefore mislead. Where possible enable the platform's high-fidelity or "original sound" mode, use a headset, and ask the client to record a sample locally and upload it for acoustic analysis.

    Privacy, consent and regulation

    • Consent: explain what telepractice involves, its benefits and limits, who will be present at each end, whether sessions are recorded, and what happens if the connection fails. Record consent. For children, consent comes from the parent or guardian, with the child's assent.
